Key Idea

The ten tasks are not ten of the same thing. Tasks 1 and 7 are creative and reflective, judged on honesty and on the selection of a significant moment. Task 2 is a performance text, judged on whether the value appears under pressure. Tasks 3, 4, 6 and 8 are articles, judged on argument and example. Task 5 is a case study, judged on analysis. Task 9 is a documented life history, judged on accuracy. Task 10 is analytical, judged on whether qualities are tied to behaviour. Writing to the wrong standard is the commonest way to lose the three marks available on a task.

1.1 Writing Task 1 — Monthly Creative Writing on Classroom Experience and Life Situations

Write every month on your classroom experience, on an observed life situation, and on the ideas and issues arising from them, to develop capacity for reflection. This is the spine of the paper; the other nine tasks are amplifications of it. A month of teaching is two hundred small experiences, and the skill being built is selection — noticing which forty seconds carried the meaning.

A good entry opens with a time, a place and a person, then moves from what happened to what it meant. Situations outside school count: a queue at a ration shop, a neighbour’s illness, a festival watched from a balcony. Keep entries under 300 words, write on the day if you can, and end with one line beginning “I did not notice that…” — the omitted observation is usually the more useful half.

1.2 Writing Task 2 — Script on Personal and Social Values, for Performance as Role-Play

Write a script on personal and social values — honesty, courage, co-operation and respect among them — that may be performed as a role-play. The operative word is may: a script read aloud and never staged has failed the task. A play about honesty is worthless if honesty is merely announced. Put the honest character something to lose, give the dishonest character a reason the audience will half-believe, and let the cost of the dishonest choice fall on someone else in the scene. Keep the language of the classroom; in a multilingual Assamese-medium school a script that moves between languages is realistic. End with a discussion question, not a moral.

1.3 Writing Task 3 — Article on Self-Understanding as Understanding One’s Own Thoughts and Actions

The syllabus supplies the definition to build on: self-understanding is the ability to understand one’s own thoughts and actions. The phrase does two pieces of work. Thoughts covers the internal half — beliefs, assumptions, expectations, the stories one tells oneself about people. Actions covers the external half — what one says and does, including what one does without noticing. An article of this kind should move from definition to mechanism to consequence.

In most adults thought and action come apart, and the gap is where difficulty lies: a person sincerely holds a value and acts against it under pressure. What closes the gap is noticing, made regular by journal writing, honest feedback and the Johari Window. Then give consequences in both directions — without self-understanding a teacher mistakes a child’s withdrawal for indifference and escalates; with it, the same teacher asks a second question. Close on a defensible claim: self-understanding is necessary but not sufficient, because insight without technique stays private.

Common Mistake

Do not define self-understanding as loving yourself, or as feeling good about yourself. That is a mood; understanding is a capacity. Define it as accurate knowledge of one’s own thoughts, feelings, motives and patterns of action, together with the ability to report them honestly, and the rest of the article follows from the definition.

1.4 Writing Task 4 — Article on Peace and Stress Management

The syllabus holds two ideas together deliberately: peace and stress management, both directed at the harmonious development of the self. Inner peace is not the absence of stress; it is the capacity to remain functional, fair and kind while under stress. Structure the article around three questions. What is stress? The body’s and mind’s response to a demand perceived as exceeding current resources — and the perception is the operative part, since the same deadline energises one student-teacher and crushes another.

What are the sources? For a D.El.Ed. student-teacher: performance anxiety in a demonstration lesson, comparison with peers, uncertainty about placement and examinations, workload, family expectation, financial strain, and difficulty speaking one’s home language in the institution. Being specific about your own stressors is half the article. What can be done? Techniques, not slogans.

1.5 Writing Task 5 — Case Study on Personal and Professional Growth

The case is not the point; the case is the evidence. You are showing that you can study one stretch of your own conduct closely enough to see the pattern inside it, and that a change in behaviour followed. Use the standard sequence. Background — the situation, with the context that makes it intelligible. Problem — stated as a behaviour, not a feeling: “I answered the child before he had finished”, not “I was impatient”. Evidence — what happened, and what others observed.

Intervention — what you actually changed, step by step. Outcome — with evidence, honestly stated if the evidence is thin. Learning — the generalisable insight, stated as a class of situations and not one incident. Include at least one instance where the intervention did not work: a case in which everything improved on the first attempt reads as reconstruction rather than research, and self-understanding includes knowing the limits of one’s own successes.

1.6 Writing Task 6 — Article on Appreciation from Others

This task inverts the usual direction of attention: you write about the ways other people have noticed something about you, and how that appreciation feeds self-understanding. A remark from another person is often the only mirror that shows a part of oneself that one’s own looking-glass cannot display — precisely the blind spot of the Johari Window. Treat three things. How appreciation works: it is not flattery, which is indiscriminate; it is specific, based on observation, and costs the speaker something.

“When you corrected Rahim in front of the class he stopped coming, so now I tell him privately and he comes back” carries information; “you are a good teacher” carries almost none. How it can be received badly — a compliment that seems to carry an expectation, praise a person feels is misplaced, feedback mistaken for criticism. How to seek it usefully — ask a specific, answerable question (“what should I stop doing in class?”) rather than an open invitation. Ground the article in two or three named sources, and write about the others with care.

1.7 Writing Task 7 — Introspective Report on a Significant Life Event

Take a significant experience or event of your life — the syllabus says explicitly good or bad — note its outcome, and write an introspective report. The difference between an anecdote and an introspective report is the whole task: an anecdote narrates, an introspective report examines your own part in what happened — what you thought, felt, wanted, did, would do differently, and now believe. The event need not be dramatic; the requirement is significance to you. Write the event first, as it happened, without evaluating it, and only then write what it meant — students who reverse this order produce justification instead of reflection.

Reflection

If the event was bad, do not spend the report on blame. Blame is externalised self-knowledge and teaches nothing. Name your own decisions, your own part and your own omission, accurately and without theatre, then state what you now do differently. If the event was good, be equally careful: the growth lies in identifying which of your own actions contributed to it, because only those can be repeated.

1.8 Writing Task 8 — “Understanding Oneself is Power”, with Examples

Take the claim seriously rather than asserting it. Begin by being precise about power: not authority over others, but capacitance — the ability to choose one’s response, to act from intention rather than impulse, and to influence a situation because one understands the forces in it. Then argue in three movements. Power over oneself: the gap between stimulus and response. A child is disrespectful; there is a choice in what happens next, and its size is a function of self-understanding.

Power in relationships: self-understanding makes accurate listening possible, because you can hear what a child says without the interference of your own unfinished business, and it makes a person coachable. Power in decision and leadership: a person who has examined their motives can be trusted, and trust is the currency of every group. Close honestly: insight not followed by practice remains insight, and real power shows up in what is done for others.

1.11 Writing Task 10 — Qualities of a Teacher and Leadership Style

Two distinctions keep this essay on track. The first is between a quality and a behaviour: “is compassionate” is a quality, “answers a child’s question before the child loses interest” is a behaviour, and only the second can be taught, observed or improved. The second is between a teacher and a leader: a teacher is judged largely by what happens in the classroom, a leader by what happens to the institution and the people in it. A teacher can be excellent and still not be a leader. Subject knowledge is necessary and not sufficient; the qualities that discriminate here are humility before evidence, emotional steadiness, procedural fairness, capacity for delegation, collegiality and accountability.

2.1 The Six Constructs of Self

The course objectives name four constructs — self-awareness, self-acceptance, self-confidence and self-motivation — and the workshops add self-esteem and self-efficacy. They are used loosely and they are not the same thing. They are also a sequence, not a list: awareness without acceptance produces insight and misery; acceptance without confidence produces comfort and passivity; confidence without motivation produces a start and no continuation; motivation without esteem produces effort without resilience, because the person reads every setback as a verdict on their worth. Efficacy is the machinery underneath all five — the belief that this particular action can be carried out in this particular situation.

Two deserve a word each. Self-confidence is a judgment of capability in a specific domain, held before the performance and based on evidence; its commonest loss is not failure but the belief that one has failed, which is why it grows most reliably out of repaired failures. Self-acceptance has two halves and people manage only one: acceptance of the given (body, history, aptitudes, temperament) and acceptance of the actual (one’s uncomfortable thoughts and impulses, not edited out of awareness). The second is harder and more useful, because an impulse never acknowledged cannot be managed — it simply appears as behaviour.

2.2 Workshop 1 (COMPULSORY) — Understanding Self and SWOT Analysis

This workshop has two parts. The first, Understanding Self, is what Section 2.1 prepares you for. The second, SWOT Analysis, may be applied personally, institutionally or organisationally, or to any other activity with reference to the functioning of the school — in practice you will do a personal SWOT and one organisational SWOT, most often of the school you are attached to or of a function in it such as the management of mid-day meals or the running of the science period.

SWOT is a general framework for examining any situation. Its logic is a two-by-two. The first axis separates what is internal to the unit being examined from what is external to it; the second separates what is helpful to a stated goal from what is harmful to it.

Two rules make a SWOT worth doing. First, name the goal before you start, because the four boxes are empty without one. “My strengths” is not a SWOT; “for the goal of taking a mixed-ability class of forty through a unit of mathematics without relying on the textbook exercise” is. Second, only internal things go in Strengths and Weaknesses. A trait, a resource, a behaviour and a skill are internal. A policy, an examination board, a change in the neighbourhood, a parent’s attitude is external. Students routinely put “good teachers are available” in Opportunities; it belongs in Strengths if it is a feature of the institution, and is more likely a Threat if the teachers are leaving.

Applied to a person, the internal axis covers temperament, habits, knowledge, skills, health, time, emotional state, reputation, and the people and materials at one’s disposal. Applied to a school, it covers the head teacher and staff, the building, the enrolled strength and the profile of the children, the materials and routines, and the internal culture; the external axis covers the Block resource persons, the state and central schemes and their timelines, the examination requirements, the community’s expectations, connectivity, and the demographic situation.

Remember

A SWOT that stops at four lists is a diagnosis, not a plan. Convert it to TOWS by matching the internal factors against the external ones: SO deploy a strength to seize an opening; ST use a strength to reduce a threat; WO close a weakness so an opportunity can be used; WT remove a weakness or limit the exposure. A personal SWOT must be written for a stated goal, never for “my life” or “my career” — the narrower the goal, the more possible it is afterwards to say whether the analysis was correct.

2.3 Workshop 2 — Life Skill Education: Meaning, Importance and Mode of Transaction

Definition

Life Skill Education is the planned and purposeful teaching of those abilities that enable a person to understand and manage the self, to make informed decisions, to communicate effectively, to work with others, to cope with stress and conflict, and to respond constructively to the situations of everyday life. It is not the teaching of a subject, but of the abilities the subject syllabus alone does not build.

The syllabus also states that conceptual understanding of LSE must be initiated before student-teachers go to the schools for the internship, that they must then practise life-skills activities involving children, and that they must submit reports. The 21st Century Skills Handbook published through the Manodarpan portal of the Ministry of Education and the Assam Life Skills Education Framework are the reference points the syllabus names.

A primary school teaches a child how many sentences there are in a paragraph, and is right to do so. It is equally the school’s responsibility to teach that child what to do when two friends quarrel, how to say no to being given something to carry, how to recognise a lie in an advertisement, what to do with rage, and how to ask a teacher for help. Those are learnable abilities, they are not taught by accident, and no subject teacher will teach them in the ordinary course.

The fifteen skills below are the ones most commonly listed in Indian school-level life-skills frameworks. The syllabus does not prescribe a closed list, so treat this as the working repertoire from which you select according to the need you have identified in a class.

Nearly every life skill is in one of two conditions: a child is managing the self, or the child is managing the self in company with others. Once you see that you can diagnose a class quickly. A class in which the recurring problem is escalation — hitting, shouting, refusing — is usually short of emotional skills: coping, empathy, assertiveness. A class in which the recurring problem is paralysis — nobody begins, nobody shares, nothing is finished — is usually short of social skills: cooperation, communication, time management. The remedy differs, and choosing the wrong one wastes a term.

Four reasons make LSE matter: it is preventive, since most of what schools later manage as indiscipline or anxiety has roots in skills never taught; it is the transferable part of learning, since a child who can lose a game and keep going can also be told that a piece of writing is not good enough yet; it is central to the Indian curriculum language, with NEP 2020, NCF-SE 2023 and the 21st Century Skills Handbook all naming social-emotional and cognitive skills among the priority competencies; and it is the teacher’s own preparation, since a teacher who has not practised managing their own emotion cannot teach a child to manage it.

Mode of transaction

The distinctive feature of LSE is its mode. These are not abilities transmitted by explanation; they are acquired by doing, watching, attempting, failing and talking about what happened. Each of the modes below has a place in a 40-minute session. A life-skills session is emphatically not a lecture on being good, and not a punishment register; both are common in Indian classrooms and both are the opposite of what this course builds.

Example

A Class IV session on managing anger, 40 minutes. 0–4 min: word-association in pairs — one child names a feeling, the partner names what the body does; collect four on the board without comment. 4–10 min: a local story of two siblings, one refused something and hits the other; stop before the resolution and ask for three alternatives. 10–14 min: model one technique only — the pause of counting to five and putting the hand down, once badly and once well. 14–24 min: role-play in threes with cards carrying different anger situations, roles rotated every round, the class naming which technique was used.

24–33 min: the class agrees three things a Class IV child can do when angry and scribes them onto a chart displayed for the year — the teacher writes their words, not four of her own. 33–38 min: where else anger shows up — the playground, the tap queue, the bus, home. 38–40 min: everyone writes one private sentence, “When I am angry I will…”; collect nothing and read none aloud, because reading them out destroys the safety that made them honest. Run it twice, a week apart, changing exactly one thing; the second run is where you discover whether the class has moved.

2.4 Workshop 3 — Communication Barriers That Hinder Effective Communication

The word effective does the important work: communication has occurred when meaning has been transferred, not when words have been sent. A teacher can be fluent, loud, clear and grammatically correct and still fail entirely to communicate, and a shy teacher with poor technique can succeed. The classic transmission account — sender, encoding, channel, decoding, receiver, noise — comes from the Shannon and Weaver tradition.

Its weakness is worth naming: it treats meaning as something packaged and delivered like a parcel. It is not. Meaning is reconstructed by the receiver out of that receiver’s own experience, and the remedy is the field of experience — the overlap between what sender and receiver know and understand. The more that field overlaps, the more meaning is shared; the less, the more the receiver simply hears words.

Definition

A communication barrier is any factor — physical, physiological, psychological, semantic, cultural, organisational or linguistic — that distorts a message, reduces the receiver’s attention to it, or causes the receiver to interpret it differently from the sender’s intention. It is a property of the situation, not of the speaker, which is why the same explanation can be clear to one person and unintelligible to another in the same room.

Active listening and the 3H technique

Almost every barrier above is reduced by one thing: the willingness and capacity to listen before responding. Active listening is not agreement and not silence; it is the deliberate effort to reconstruct the speaker’s meaning, check that reconstruction, and only then respond. Its observable components are facing the speaker, allowing silence, asking open questions, reflecting back, paraphrasing and summarising. A widely used teaching device in Indian teacher-training material is the 3H technique — Head, Heart, Hand.

A message may be head-received (understood: I know what was said), heart-received (felt: I take it seriously, and I understand the feeling behind it), and hand-received (acted upon: something is actually done). A message can stop at any of the three, and each stopping point is a different failure. “Your drawing is untidy” is head-received and nowhere else, and usually stops the drawing; agreement without change is the commonest outcome with school administrators. Indian teacher-training resources use several closely related listening frameworks with different acronym expansions; the 3H sequence is used here because it is simple, teachable, and maps directly onto the three failure modes.

Worked case. A Class III child’s father tells the teacher at the gate that the child is being beaten at home, and asks that nothing be said to the mother. The barrier is organisational and cultural — the relationship between family and school, and the convention that family matters are not the school’s business. The head-received failure is registering the words but not the fear. The heart-received response is asking what the child says at home, and asking again what the father wants to happen now. The hand-received response is deciding what action follows, including referral to the child protection mechanisms and the school-based child protection committee studied in S1.1. The first part is a communication task, the second a professional and ethical one, and they cannot be separated.

2.5 Workshop 4 — Professional Ethics and Accountability of Teachers

These two are taught together because in teaching the second is what gives the first teeth. A teacher with strong ethical commitments who is never answerable to anyone is ethical in intention only; a teacher who is closely supervised but has never reflected on what is right is compliant, not professional. A profession is a group holding specialised knowledge, bound to a code of conduct, accountable to a community of practice rather than to one employer, and accepting a duty to keep competence current — all four are present in teaching.

A professional teacher is one who meets that standard in practice, and note the word effect: a teacher can be technically correct, punctual and even liked and still cause harm through shaming, through ridicule of a child’s home language, through public comparison of children, or through silence about a child in distress.

Definition

Professional ethics is the set of principles governing professional conduct — the standards of behaviour a person is expected to meet because of the role they hold, not merely because of law or convenience. Professional accountability is the fact of being answerable, to identified parties, for the discharge of that role, which implies their right to know, to question and to require correction.

In India the reference point is the Teacher Professional Code of Ethics issued by the National Council for Teacher Education (NCTE), the statutory body established under the NCTE Act, 1993. NCTE also notifies a Professional Ethics Committee, the body through which matters of professional ethics are considered. The Code is organised around the dignity of the profession, then around the teacher’s dealings with learners, the school, parents and guardians, colleagues and staff, and the community.

Ethical dilemmas in the classroom

A dilemma arises when two obligations are genuinely in conflict, so that whichever course is taken one value is partly violated. Nine recur often enough to be worth rehearsing in advance, because rehearsing them is the purpose of this workshop.

  1. Favouritism. Extra attention to a child whose family is helpful to the school, at the cost of the others. The pull is social and practical; the obligation is procedural fairness.
  2. Reporting a colleague. A senior colleague has been persistently harsh to a class. Reporting protects the children and damages a relationship — and a school in which no one reports anything protects no one.
  3. Confidentiality. A child discloses something painful. The teacher is bound to act, and the child assumed the conversation was private. The conflict cannot be dissolved, only handled honestly, with the child’s knowledge wherever possible.
  4. Discipline and punishment. Corporal punishment is prohibited, and the genuine problem of a class that will not attend to instruction is not solved by removing the one available instrument. The answer is a system of consequence, consistency and restoration.
  5. The teacher’s own child. A relative is in the class. Full neutrality towards one’s own family is difficult; the workable answer is usually to disclose the relationship and agree in advance who handles difficult moments.
  6. Language and identity. A child is corrected harshly for using the home language in a class where almost everyone uses it. Efficiency and the child’s dignity are in conflict.
  7. Gifts and favour. A small gift is ordinary courtesy in some communities; in a code, accepting anything that could be read as influencing assessment is a risk. What is required is a declared, consistent, transparent rule.
  8. Use of children for personal errands. Routine and almost universal in many schools, and a clear use of institutional resources for private purposes.
  9. Photography and sharing of children’s images. A photograph posted without consent is a disclosure, and children cannot withdraw from it. This is now also a matter of law in India, and in any case a matter of dignity.

Accountability runs in six directions, and only the first is obvious. A teacher is accountable to learners for their learning, safety, dignity and fair treatment; to the school for preparation, record-keeping, the timetable and the institution’s reputation; to parents for honest information and family confidentiality; to the community for being a credible public institution; to oneself for honesty about one’s own competence, including admitting error and asking for help before a child is failed; and to the profession for not lowering its standards by one’s own practice.

The honest difficulty in each case is worth naming: compliance with the institution can itself become unethical if the institution is wrong; community expectation can be contradictory and can press the teacher against a child’s interest; and accountability to oneself is the one nobody else will check, so it is the one that decays first.

Exam Focus

A viva question on this workshop is likely to be a scenario question rather than a definition. Prepare an answer in three parts for each of the nine dilemmas: what the obligations are, which is stronger and why, and what I would actually do on Monday morning. The third part is the one that is assessed.

2.6 Workshop 5 — Meaning of Self, Factors Impacting Self-Development, and the Johari Window

Meaning of self

Three different things get called “the self” in ordinary speech. The self as identity — “who I am”: a relatively stable collection of beliefs and self-descriptions through which a person interprets experience; it can be examined, because it consists in part of assigned meanings, and meanings can be revised. The self as self-concept — “how I see myself”: not a fact but a judgement, held in head and body, formed from experience and constantly updated; a hypothesis about oneself, not a report of oneself.

The self as self-image — “how I appear to others”: inseparable in practice from the first two, because a person is not always aware of the gap between how they are and how they come across, and much of the ordinary distress of teaching arises from that gap. Because the self is a set of meanings rather than a fixed thing, it can be developed: self-development is not the accumulation of credentials, but the deliberate revision of the meanings one holds about oneself and the deliberate widening of the evidence one allows to enter them.

Factors that impact self-development

  1. Internal. Age and stage; temperament; the quality of early attachment; physical health and disability; mental habits such as perfectionism, rumination and avoidance; the accuracy of self-perception; and how success and failure are attributed — to circumstances, to others, or to one’s own controllable action.
  2. Family and social. The family’s style of expressing approval or criticism; comparison with others; the emotional climate of the home; the influence of the wider kin group; and the presence or absence of at least one adult who took the person seriously.
  3. Peer and school. Whether the school rewarded effort or only performance; ranking and public comparison; the teacher’s attitude to mistakes; sport, art and group activity; and the school’s overall climate of dignity or of shame.
  4. Cultural and social context. Family expectation, community reputation and the multiple demands on a woman’s and a man’s time alike; the multilingual classroom; and the economic situation of the family, which shapes both opportunity and self-expectation.
  5. Professional. The school’s culture; the head teacher’s style; the availability of mentoring; the possibility of practice-teaching with feedback; the demands of the internship; and institutional pressure to conform.
  6. Situational and life. Health, bereavement, relationship change, financial strain, examination seasons, and the particular stage of the D.El.Ed. year in which a self-assessment is done.

The Johari Window (from Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingold) is the most usable instrument in this paper, because it converts the vague instruction “know yourself” into two specific and quite different jobs: learning what others see, and deciding what to show. The open area is known to oneself and to others — the region of accurate mutual understanding where trust and real communication are possible, and where a teaching relationship feels effortless because little has to be explained or defended. The blind spot is known to others and unknown to oneself: the effect one has on a room that one cannot see.

It shrinks only by asking other people, and by believing what they say — which is exactly where Writing Task 6 operates. The hidden area is known to oneself and unknown to others — the fear before the demonstration lesson, the shame after a poor result, and also capacities one has never claimed; it shrinks by disclosure, to the right person, in an appropriate setting. The unknown area is known to neither oneself nor others — potential not yet discovered by anyone; it shrinks through experience, new situations and honest exposure to one’s limits.

Worked example. A student-teacher who has never received specific feedback records the blind pane as: “I did not know I interrupt; I only knew that some children stopped talking.” Two colleagues are asked one question — “when I am explaining something, what do I do that is unhelpful?” — and both answer the same thing independently. That single convergence converts a guess into knowledge, and the open area grows.

A teacher working on the hidden pane might record “I am afraid in front of the head teacher, and I have arranged to avoid the demonstration class”; the growth comes from doing the demonstration anyway, with a colleague in the room. The model transfers to children directly: apply the window to a child who has stopped speaking in class, ask what each pane would contain, and give the one action that would most enlarge that child’s open area.

2.7 Workshop 6 — Practising Mindfulness and Meditation for Harmonious Relations

Note the verb: practice, not study. This workshop cannot be run as a talk; it has to be done in the room, and the doing is the assessment.

Definition

Mindfulness is the quality of attention in which a person is present with what is happening now, without rushing to judge, approve or reject it — a quality of attention, cultivable inside or outside any formal practice. Meditation is a family of structured practices for training that attention, such as attending repeatedly to the breath, to bodily sensation or to sound, and noticing when attention has wandered and returning it. Mindfulness is what meditation is for.

Mindfulness and meditation have deep origins in several religious and contemplative traditions, and it is important to be accurate about that rather than either denying it or collapsing the traditions into a technique. In this course they are used in the secular, academic sense: as attention-training practices that can be taught in a public school without belonging to any religion. Religious practice is undertaken within a tradition, on its own terms, for the ends that the tradition defines; its value cannot be reduced to its technique, and it should neither be required of a child nor dismissed.

Secular mindfulness is used as a psychological skill, the way physical education uses running: the running is not religious, the benefit is real, and the person teaching it is not claiming to be a religious authority. In your classroom, teach the practice; do not teach the doctrine; and never require a child to take part in a practice belonging to a tradition their family does not belong to.

Simple secular practices, to teach and to practise

  • The three-minute breathing space. Sitting, attending to the breath and the body, noticing when attention has gone elsewhere and bringing it back. The return of attention is the practice, not the staying.
  • STOP. A four-step sequence for the moment of pressure: S–top, pause; T–ake a slow breath; O–bserve what is happening inside and outside; P–roceed with a chosen action rather than a reactive one. The most teachable form, because it takes four seconds and applies in a corridor.
  • Mindful listening, three minutes. The teacher reads a short passage; children listen with eyes closed and raise a hand when they notice their own mind wandering. Discovering how often attention leaves is itself the lesson.
  • The body scan for children. Attention moved slowly from the crown of the head to the feet, noticing hands, shoulders, tummy — places where a class holds its tension. Four or five minutes, in silence.
  • Mindful movement. Walking slowly across the room, feeling the foot lift, move and land. Useful with older primary children, and a good bridge to the yoga component of S1.8.
  • Noticing three things. At the end of the day or session, naming three things that went well and one difficulty met and handled — a deliberate corrective to the habit of noticing only what went wrong.

The strongest argument for these practices in an elementary school is not relaxation. A class taught by a person managing their own attention is a class in which children are taught how to manage theirs, and most of what is called “restlessness” in a primary class is a difficulty of attention rather than a moral defect. Two cautions: do not present these practices as cures. They are associated with improved attention, better emotional regulation and less reactivity, and those associations are what you may honestly describe; they are not treatment for illness, they do not promise examination results, and they do not substitute for referral, for a proper rest break, or for a conversation with a colleague about a workload that is genuinely beyond a person.

Unit 3 Seminar — Any Two (2 × 5 = 10)

Four seminar themes are listed and two are chosen. Each carries 5 marks, awarded on the basis of the report in which the student-teacher reflects on their own critical thinking. 3.1 Seminar 1 — Nurturing Humane Qualities

Humane qualities are dispositions, not skills: compassion, empathy, respect, kindness, humility, honesty, patience and a concern for the dignity of other people. They are dispositions because they cannot be acquired by instruction; they are built by the situations one is repeatedly placed in and by what those situations reward. What makes this a seminar rather than a topic is that the group has to locate the moments in ordinary teaching where these qualities are actually tested — and the honest answer is uncomfortable. It is the moment a child repeats a mistake for the fourth time; the moment a colleague is unfair to a weaker teacher in a staff meeting; the moment a parent’s complaint is defensible and cannot be met.

  • Empathy versus sympathy. Empathy is understanding another person’s experience as that person experiences it; sympathy is feeling sorry from one’s own position. Sympathy can become distance; empathy is the basis of good response.
  • The dignity clause. Being treated as a person is a condition for learning, not a reward for good behaviour. A child who is shamed stops learning before the content is reached.
  • Humility is functional. It is what makes a teacher able to be corrected, and therefore able to improve.
  • Integrity under low supervision. What a teacher does when no register is marked and no inspector is present is the useful test of humane qualities — not the practice the test expects, but the practice that is convenient.

Seminar method: case vignettes read aloud, discussed in small groups, then each group asked what the quality in question would actually cost the teacher. If the answer is “nothing”, the group has chosen the wrong vignette.

3.2 Seminar 2 — Managing Emotion

Emotion is not the problem. Unmanaged emotion is. This seminar treats managing emotion as a sequence: notice it in the body before it becomes a judgement, name it precisely, understand what it is about, choose a response rather than reacting, and only then act. Almost every failure at the last two steps is a failure at the first. The teacher who does not notice the rising heat cannot choose, and can only react.

  • Triggers are personal and often predictable. A colleague’s public correction, a child’s defiance in front of others, a long untended queue. Knowing one’s own list is the first practical outcome of this seminar.
  • The narrow window. Under rising arousal, the range of responses a person can choose from narrows. The function of a pause is not to be calm but to widen that range back before answering.
  • Rumination is not processing. Processing ends in a decision or a release; rumination is a circular loop of blame with no exit, and it looks like serious thought while producing none.
  • Other people’s emotion is data. Emotion in a class is information about the situation and about the person, not an obstacle to instruction — and suppressing the display of it without addressing the cause simply relocates the problem.
  • Sequences worth building: STOP from Workshop 6; the pause and the naming; the technique of writing the emotion out in one unedited paragraph before replying to anything difficult.

Seminar method: participants describe one real incident of lost control; the group analyses what the emotion was, what preceded it, and what a different first move could have been. Plenary closes with each participant naming one private trigger and one agreed response for it.

3.3 Seminar 3 — Inculcating Soft Skills and Social Grace

Soft skills are the learnable abilities through which a person works with others and manages their own working life — listening, speaking, cooperation, self-management, problem-solving, decision-making and, increasingly, the digital skills covered in the internship. Social grace is the courteous and workable form these take in company: greeting, waiting one’s turn, not interrupting, saying no without shaming, thanking, apologising, entering and leaving a shared space without collision. Its basis is respect for the other’s dignity and autonomy. Grace that enforces one group’s habits on another, or that punishes a child for what the home language calls polite, is not grace.

  • Soft skills are teachable; virtues are not. “Wait your turn” can be modelled, rehearsed and rewarded. “Be kind” can only be exemplified.
  • Grace cuts across language. A child who greets a teacher with folded hands, a child who greets with namaskar, a child who simply looks up and nods — all are acknowledging. Insisting on one form penalises a home language and the families who use it.
  • Grace among equals. What is taught must extend to the weakest child, to the mute child, to the newest child and to the cleaning staff of the school. Grace that is selective is status, not grace.
  • Digital conduct. Sharing photographs, forwarding messages and group membership are now part of a child’s social life, and the school’s rules about them should be discussed with children, not merely imposed on them.

Seminar method: the group lists the ten courtesies a Class I child actually needs, then asks which of them the current class routine practises and which it quietly punishes. The usual finding is that a noisy, active class is praised for speed and punished for grace, and that reversing that is cheap.

3.4 Seminar 4 — Negotiating Self-Criticism and Self-Validation for Self-Esteem

This seminar handles the two voices everyone has. One is the inner critic, which evaluates by comparison and by character — “you are not a good teacher,” “you are not that sort of person” — and which produces shame. The other is the inner coach, which evaluates by action — “that lesson moved too fast for the back three rows; tomorrow try the demonstration before the explanation” — and which produces improvement. Shame makes a person avoid the evidence; a coach makes a person go and look at it. That single difference is why self-criticism, which sounds like a virtue, can hold a person at a fixed level of performance.

Table 3.1 — Negotiation Table for the Self-Criticism Seminar
Self-critical statementCoach’s re-statementWhat the re-statement makes possible
“I am a failure as a teacher.”“That lesson did not reach the back three rows. Here is what I will change on Monday.”A specific change, a test of it, and a repeat
“They all think I am incompetent.”“Two of them asked me to repeat it; the rest said nothing, which tells me less than I assume.”Checking a belief instead of obeying it
“I should be able to do this without thinking.”“Expert teaching uses routines built by deliberate practice, not by absence of thought.”Planning as competence rather than as failure

Self-validation is the deliberate provision of evidence to oneself, so that self-esteem does not depend entirely on who else happens to be present. Its sources are realistic rather than inflated: the record of work actually done, progress actually made, promises actually kept, feedback actually received and acted on. Low self-esteem and high self-esteem share the same defect from opposite ends — both are unstable under ordinary contact with evidence, because both depend on something outside the person. Validation is the internalisation of that evidence.

School Internship Programme Life-Skills Activities with School Children

The SIP component of this paper requires the student-teacher, during the school internship, to conduct life-skills activities with school children and to submit a report on them. It is the point at which the whole paper stops being a report about oneself and becomes a report about what happened in a classroom. Nothing new is taught here: the six constructs, the fifteen life skills, the nine modes of transaction and the six-stage shape of a session have all been prepared in the preceding pages.

Table 4.1 — The SIP Task, Step by Step
StepWhat to doWhat to record
1. DiagnoseSit in on two or three ordinary periods of a class you will work with. Note one recurring difficulty, not a dramatic one — escalation, silence, non-finishing, turn-takingClass, date, period, the difficulty in one sentence, and one quotation of what actually happened
2. SelectChoose the one life skill that addresses that difficulty. More than one skill in a forty-minute session with children who have never done this before guarantees that none of it landsThe skill, and the reasoning from observed behaviour to selected skill in three or four sentences
3. PlanWrite the six stages — warm-up, model, practice, reflect, transfer, assess — with minutes against each, and the mode of transaction for each stage. Decide what you will change if only one thing survivesThe plan, and the fallback if the class will not participate
4. PrepareArrange materials, a space that is not rows-and-desks, and permission. Tell the class teacher what you are doing and why; tell the children only that something different is going to happenMaterials list, time and place agreed
5. ConductRun the session. Keep to the plan for the first two stages; after that, follow the class. Expect to be wrong about the timeActual minutes at each stage against planned; two things that did not go as intended
6. RepeatRun a second session with one change only. A single session proves nothing about the class; the comparison is the evidenceWhat you changed and the difference you observed

Reflection

The report is at its best when it is uncomfortable. “The children enjoyed it” and “the activity was successful” carry no information. The lines that earn marks are the observational ones — by the third round the argument had stopped; a child who had not spoken in a week spoke once and then withdrew again; I had underestimated how long permission to speak takes — followed by the decision those observations forced. Write what you saw, then what you will do differently, then why you will do it.

Precautions. Do not conduct a life-skills session as a substitute for the subject periods you are attached to, and do not make a child’s participation in it a condition of anything. Never use the session to discipline, to name children publicly, or to correct academic errors — the moment a session is used as punishment it teaches the opposite of what it is for. Do not record individual children by name or photograph them without permission. Keep the activity short: two sessions, not ten, and the second one matters more than the first.

Common Mistake

The total of the three units is 40 (15 + 15 + 10), not 50. The syllabus specifies 50 internal marks for the paper but does not name the remaining 10 as a separate component; the SIP component — life-skills activities with school children and a report on them, listed in this subject’s syllabus and also carried in SI-I — accounts for exactly 10, which is why it is shown in the table above. This reading is inferred, not stated: if your institution’s notification or the mentor’s instruction allocates those 10 marks differently, follow the institution. What must not be done is to present a 40-mark structure as the full scheme, or to add a component the syllabus never mentions.

Because assessment is internal, the standard applied is the standard of the report, not of the examination hall. Unit 1 is marked on what was written and on whether it shows the writer’s own thinking; Unit 2 on the reflection on critical thinking the workshop was designed to elicit; Unit 3 on the same basis. Nothing in this subject rewards a student for describing what self-understanding is in general, and everything in it rewards a student for describing what it looks like in one specific classroom, on one specific day, with evidence.

Mode of Transaction

The syllabus states that the nature of the course is practical, in the form of writing, workshop and seminar, and that it is to be exclusively monitored and evaluated at the respective Teacher Education Institute. It is designed so that student-teachers assimilate and internalise the situations they are placed in rather than memorising descriptions of them. Concretely, the course is transacted through:

  • Workshops and seminars conducted with contextualised material and formats, prepared by the mentor and the faculty team.
  • Various types of writing tasks — creative writing, article writing, script writing, case study and introspective report — submitted across the semester.
  • Monthly journal writing, which is a continuous reflective practice rather than a single submission.
  • Group discussion, sharing and interaction with experts, faculties and mentors.
  • Audio-visual devices — film, CD, documentary, or the reading of a text — used to open a seminar or workshop before discussion begins.
  • Interactive sessions, group discussion, role play and presentation inside the workshop and seminar format.
  • School internship and visits, including a visit to a meditation centre or spiritual development centre in the local area, which the syllabus notes may provide an additional opportunity for understanding the self.

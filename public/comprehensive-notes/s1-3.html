Unit 1Language and Communication in Society

Unit 1 is the conceptual base of the paper. Before asking how language is learned, it asks what language is and why societies cannot do without it. The four adjectives in 1.3 — dynamic, creative, arbitrary, perpetual — describe four properties that explain almost everything else in this paper, including why a home language cannot simply be replaced by a school language.

1.1 Nature of Language; Nature of Knowledge

Language is a system of communication — a definition that is correct as far as it goes, but incomplete in a way that matters for a teacher, because it leaves out the two things language actually does: it represents thought, and it creates thought. Every human language has four basic properties. It uses a limited set of sounds, or phonemes, and combines them systematically; it combines those sounds into meaningful units called words; it arranges words into sentences according to rules of order and combination; and it attaches meaning to the result and to the manner of using it.

Knowledge, in the sense used in this paper, means organised, shareable and verifiable information: knowing that a seed grows, that water boils at a certain temperature, that two and two make four. Two properties of it are decisive. Knowledge is cumulative — it accumulates and is handed down, so that each generation begins with the work of the last. And it is collaborative — a person alone in a forest may discover that fire is useful, but the useful knowledge of fire is the socially stored, socially transmitted kind.

Definition

Language is a conventional and systematic symbol system used by a human community for exchanging messages, for representing thought and for creating meaning — made of sounds and written symbols, governed by rules of combination and equally by conventions of use.

Human beings do not think in symbols and then wrap meaning around them; they think in language. L. S. Vygotsky, in Thought and Language (1934), put the matter at its strongest: the higher mental functions — abstract reasoning, planning, self-awareness, voluntary attention — have no natural existence apart from language. Words are not labels pasted on ideas that exist independently; concepts such as fair, cruel or possible have no meaning outside a language, because a word is a concept and a cultural object at once. The stock of ideas a speaker owns is bounded by the stock of words that speaker owns.

The implication for a primary teacher is direct. If concept and word are bound together, a child still acquiring words in a second language is also still acquiring the concepts those words carry. Telling a Class II child that the Assamese word for rain and the English word rain name the same object does not give the child the idea of rain; the idea is built through the language in which the surrounding adults discuss it. This is the strongest argument for teaching the early years in the child's mother tongue — and it is an argument from the nature of knowledge, not from sentiment.

1.2 Need of Language for Acquiring Other Knowledge

Nothing in a school happens except through language. The child's first acquisition of any subject is linguistic. Before a child can count they must know the number words; before they can add they must understand the words of the operation; before they can understand that a plant needs water they must understand the sentences in which the fact is embedded. Subject knowledge does not sit beside language in a school timetable; it arrives through it, and in an early primary class it is it.

Look at the last row. Where the child does not yet control the medium of instruction, an examination measures the child's language, and marks attributed to the subject are really marks attributed to language. The practical consequence is a language-sensitive pedagogy: teach the subject in the child's language first, and treat the school language as a second acquisition rather than as the gate through which the subject must pass.

A second reason the need is absolute is that knowledge itself is inherited through language. No child invents the alphabet, its value system or the number system; each generation receives a compressed account of the past in words and enlarges it. When a child asks, as children do, who made words?, the honest answer is that nobody did in any final sense — words were made by long collective use. That is precisely why language is a social object and not a private one.

1.3 Language is Dynamic, Creative, Arbitrary and Perpetual

The syllabus compresses four properties of language into one line, and each produces a different classroom consequence.

Dynamic means that language is alive and changes. New words enter it because new things need naming; old words change or disappear because the things they named have gone. A Class III teacher in Assam meets this directly: a child brings the word laptop or solar inverter and the textbook has never seen it. Because language is dynamic, no fixed word list is ever finished, and a teacher who treats the textbook's vocabulary as the total vocabulary of the language is teaching a dead language.

Creative means that language is not a closed inventory but a rule-governed capacity. A speaker who has never heard the word unbelievable can produce it correctly from the un- + believe + -able pattern. Combinatorial productivity — a limited number of units yielding an unlimited number of meaningful wholes — is what makes language a system rather than a list, and it is why teaching patterns is more useful than teaching lists.

Arbitrary means that there is no necessary connection between the form of a word and what it means. The word water does not contain water, does not sound like water, and could have been called anything. A language's signs are conventional, and only convention holds them steady. Some signs are motivated — onomatopoeic, or iconic in gesture — but the system as a whole rests on convention, and it is arbitrary in a practically important sense: a child cannot infer meaning from a printed page, because print is the most arbitrary form of all. This is the theoretical reason why reading has to be taught and cannot be left to happen.

Perpetual means that language is never finished and never finishes. Its perpetuity is the direct result of the other three: because it is arbitrary it can grow, because it is dynamic it does grow, and because it is creative the growth is unlimited combination. For the teacher, perpetuity is an argument for humility about the textbook and for treating children as contributors. A Class IV child who brings a new word or a new construction is not making a mistake in the language; the language is doing what it has always done.

Common Mistake

Do not answer "language is arbitrary" as "language is meaningless". Arbitrariness means there is no natural resemblance between sign and referent; it does not mean a sign has no meaning. A sign has meaning because a community agrees that it has meaning, and that agreement is binding within the community.

1.4 Language as an Integral Part of Culture and a Mark of Cultural Identity and Heritage

Culture is the whole of a community's way of life — food, dress, dwelling, work, festivals, religious practice, music, forms of address, rules of politeness. Language is integral to this in a double sense. It records culture, in proverbs, folk tales, songs, the names of plants and animals, kinship terms, and words for weaving and fishing. And it carries culture: the manner of speaking itself enacts culture, since forms of address in Assamese differ by age and relationship, certain intonations carry politeness, and the choice of code between two speakers is itself a cultural act.

Consider a small set of words that cannot be borrowed without loss: a mother, a grandmother, a younger sister, a rice-paddy, the first-flood rains, a particular loom, a particular folk song. These words mark a way of life. A community that stops using them does not merely lose vocabulary; it loses the practice, and then the memory, because once the name is gone the custom becomes hard to describe. Language is therefore a storehouse of cultural heritage and a record of a community's history — the history of a community is written in its proverbs, place names, oral traditions and loan words, which show who traded with whom and worshipped what.

From this follows the weight of cultural identity. Language is a mark of identity because it is one of the last and most visible signs of who a person belongs to: a person's name, mother tongue, script and idiom travel with them, and drop them into an unfamiliar linguistic setting and they are marked as outsiders. When a school takes away the language a child brings, it is making a statement about who in the community is at home. This is why NEP 2020 and NCF-SE 2023 place the home language at the centre of the foundational stage, and why the folk-tale activity in this paper's practicum is not decoration: bringing a folk tale or folk song into the classroom is an act of recognition.

A corresponding warning belongs here. Cultures are not sealed boxes, and a culture is not a museum. Language change is not cultural loss, and borrowing is not betrayal: the Jughon with Mising, or the tea-garden worker who speaks Sadri at home and Assamese in the bazaar, is using a repertoire. What damages culture is not plurality but the disappearance of opportunities to use a language in public — no school that will teach it, no signboard that will use it, no elder left to ask.

1.5 Language as a Tool of Self-Expression

The fifth point is the most personal. Self-expression is the process by which a person makes an inner state available to others and thereby to themselves. Language is the ordinary tool of the process, but it is a tool with a particular shape: it works by selecting. To speak is to choose a word from among many, and so to select one perspective rather than another. A child who says "the garhi is broken" is making a statement; one who says "she broke my pen" is making a claim; one who says "I could not finish it" is making an excuse. The grammar lesson is the ethics lesson: language is where thinking becomes visible and therefore available to challenge.

Language also shapes the self-conception available to a child. A child who is spoken to warmly at home, whose jokes are laughed at, whose questions are answered seriously, grows up with a self that speaks. A child whose language is ridiculed, or whose questions are met with "don't speak, we can't understand", learns to be silent. Silencing is not only the loss of a vocabulary; it is the loss of a self that is permitted to exist. This is the most important reason a teacher must notice how children speak to one another, and not only what is said.

For the elementary classroom this has three consequences. Expression needs a repertoire: a child with a small vocabulary can express want ("more", "water", "no") but not reasoning ("because", "if", "instead"), so a programme of language development is also a programme of reasoning. Expression needs a listener: telling, asking, explaining, discussing and singing must be built into the daily routine.

Expression needs permission: a classroom in which only the confident child is answered and the quiet one overlooked is producing a silent half of the class. This is why the compulsory practicum activity — debate, extempore speech, storytelling, role play — is on the syllabus: debate requires a position and a reason, extempore requires thinking while speaking, storytelling requires sequencing, voice and audience, and role play requires the child to stand outside their own view.

Unit 2Language Acquisition and Learning

Unit 1 described language as a social object. Unit 2 turns to the individual: how a child comes to have a language, in what order the pieces arrive, and what happens to a child whose first language is not the language of the school. It also gives the technical content of the syllabus — the structure of language, phonology, phonics and morphology — as a set of rules and patterns teachable from a text.

2.1 Language and the Child

Every child is born with a capacity for language, but not with a particular language. What the child has at birth is a biological preparedness: an ear that can discriminate the sounds of human speech, a vocal apparatus that can produce them, and a brain that detects regularities in a stream of sound and stores them. Between roughly six and twelve months a child enters the babbling period. This matters for the theory of acquisition, because the sounds the child produces are not random: the child favours, at the earliest stage, the sounds of the language the child is hearing. A child hearing only Assamese produces Assamese-like syllables; a child hearing Bengali produces Bengali-like syllables. The environment selects from a shared human repertoire.

Between one and two years the child produces single words, and the first words are a signal of what a word is for: the child who says milk is not practising a sound but referring to something wanted or present. Shortly afterwards come two-word combinations, and the crucial observation — made first by Beatrice Skelland Beadle and Mary Agassi in the 1930s and used ever since — is that these are the first sign of grammar. The child says more milk (modifier before noun), open door (verb before object), all gone. The child has never been taught the rule; the child has deduced that the language orders units, and is applying the rule to a new word.

Between two and six years the child is in the pre-scholastic stage of language, and the range of growth is enormous: vocabulary expands rapidly, sentences lengthen, and questions, negation, tense, comparison, sequence and condition appear. The child also shifts from talking about things to talking about talk. That shift, metalinguistic awareness, is the foundation of literacy, of grammar teaching, and of the school's ability to explain language at all. In a multilingual environment the child does not acquire a confused mixture; the child sorts the languages by situation and interlocutor. Talking to a grandmother produces one repertoire, talking to a shopkeeper another, a school teacher a third. This code-switching is not confusion but an awareness of social context, and it is a resource.

Two things follow for the classroom. The child arrives with a substantial language system, whatever the school thinks of it, so the teacher's task is extension rather than installation. And the early years are the years in which a child's language is most vulnerable — to ridicule, to correction of accent, to the assumption that a language other than the school's is a nuisance.

2.2 Home Language, School Language and the Transition from One to the Other

The home language (matribhasa, literally "mother language") is the language a child acquires first, naturally and without formal teaching, in ordinary family life. The school language is the language in which the school teaches, in which the textbook is written, in which the teacher explains and in which the child is examined. For an Assamese-medium school in Assam the school language is normally Assamese. The two are frequently different, and the whole of 2.2 is about the gap between them.

Table 2.1 — Home language and school language compared

The two are not rivals; they have different jobs. The home language gives the child the linguistic scaffolding — everyday talk, questions, stories, quarrels and songs — within which concepts are first formed. The school language gives access to the printed curriculum. A programme that discards the first in a hurry to install the second destroys the scaffolding and then finds the child cannot climb.

The transition from the home language to the school language is a period, not a moment. During it the child shows a reduction in the amount and quality of talk: understands a great deal but answers in short phrases, reverts to the home language when the school language fails, avoids tasks needing speech, and may appear to have regressed. A well-informed teacher reads this as the temporary stage of a bilingual child, not as a child who cannot learn.

Three things make the transition go well: time, two or three years of sustained exposure; understandable input, slower, more literal speech tied to things the child can see; and continued use of the home language. Withdrawing a home language early does not speed the transition — it removes the child's confidence, and the school language is then learned more slowly, not faster.

2.3 Acquisition of Language Skills; Foundational Literacy and Numeracy

A child acquires a language skill in stages, and the stages are largely invariant across languages, though the pace is not. The sequence runs: pre-speaking (0–12 months) → one-word stage (about 12–18 months) → two-word stage (about 18–24 months) → the stage of basic structures (roughly 2–5 years, when plurals, possessives, tenses, questions, negation, commands and multi-clause sentences appear) → the stage of complex structures (roughly 5 years onward, when passives, conditionals and embedded clauses are mastered).

Reading and writing come later and differently, because they are not a continuation of speech but a new system built on the old one. The child must first learn to decode — to convert a printed symbol into the sound of the language, the bridge discussed in 2.4 — and then to encode. Emergent writing appears before formal writing: a child draws a map-like squiggle for a car, then repeated marks, then individual letters, then strings of letters, then words with correct spelling, and only then does writing correspond to intended speech. A teacher who expects handwriting on the first day of Class I is expecting the last stage of a process the child has not begun.

This is where Foundational Literacy and Numeracy enters. NEP 2020 gives FLN a decisive place, identifying it as the first of the priority areas for the foundational stage, on the ground that a child who cannot read, write and count cannot do anything else in school.

Definition

Foundational Literacy and Numeracy (FLN) is the early stage of schooling in which every child acquires the basic capabilities of reading, writing, speaking and number, to the level required for further learning in school and beyond. Under NEP 2020 it is the first of the priority areas for the foundational stage.

The national mission is NIPUN Bharat — the National Initiative for Proficiency in Numeracy and Foundational Literacy — whose overarching guidelines were released by the Ministry of Education in 2022. It is not a syllabus point of this paper but the policy framework in which 2.3 sits, and its most useful feature for classroom purposes is the specification of twelve high-priority competencies across three domains: these competencies, rather than the annual class, are the real targets of the early years.

Read against the classroom, the three language competencies are the ones this paper is about. Oral skills is the foundation, largely acquired before school and so undervalued; a child who cannot explain a picture in words will not write an answer. Decoding is where phonology and phonics enter — marks on a page are codes for sounds, and sounds are codes for words. Encoding is the reverse, growing out of what the child can already say. The three cannot be separated. Numeracy sits in the same list for a parallel reason — a child who has memorised 2 + 2 = 4 has learned a string of words, not a relation.

2.4 Structure of Language, Phonics and Phonology

This sub-point and 2.5 both mention phonics and phonology, and the difference between them is exactly the difference between the sound level and the word level of language. 2.4 concerns the structure of language as a whole and the system of sounds within it; 2.5 concerns how words are built out of meaningful parts. Phonics appears in 2.4 as the bridge from print to sound, and returns in 2.5 as the input from which the child detects those meaningful parts.

A language can be described at several levels, and each asks a different question. Phonetics studies sounds as actually produced; phonology studies them as a system — which sounds the language treats as distinct, and which sequences are permitted; morphology studies the smallest meaningful units; syntax the arrangement of words; semantics meaning; pragmatics meaning in use.

Phonology and the phoneme

The central unit of phonology is the phoneme — the smallest unit of sound in a language that can change the meaning of a word. In bat, pat, rat and mat the initial consonant does essential work: it changes the meaning while the rest stays the same. Sounds that distinguish words in this way are contrastive or phonemic.

Other speech sounds, such as the clear l at the end of milk where the grammar expects a nasal, are allophones, or predictable variants, and the child does not treat them as separate units. A language's phoneme inventory is therefore finite and countable — typically between about twenty and several dozen units — whereas the number of words is enormous. This efficiency is what makes the phoneme, not the letter or the word, the true atom of spoken language.

Phonics is the practical counterpart of phonology: the method by which a child is taught the relationship between the graphemes (written letters and letter groups) and the phonemes they represent, converting the arbitrary symbol on the page into a sound and therefore a meaning. Because the correspondence is not one-to-one, phonics is a set of recurring patterns rather than a single code. It also has to be learnt in the language the child is actually reading, which is one more reason that beginning with a language the child speaks makes decoding an ordinary task instead of a translation task.

Classroom Connection

An early phonics routine for a multilingual Class I or II: say the word, have the class say it, tap the sounds while you write the letters, blend back into the word, read it in a short text. Start with words whose sound–spell relationship is regular and hold irregular words back. The usual sequence is letters in a meaningful context first (the child's own name), then three-phoneme words with continuous sounds, then a single vowel pattern, then harder blends and digraphs, and irregular words last. What is not to be done is the opposite — treating the alphabet as the first content of the language period. A child who recites the alphabet fluently and cannot blend three letters into a word has memorised a sequence, not acquired a system.

Moving up the hierarchy, the three components a primary teacher most often needs to name are syntax, the arrangement of words; lexis, the vocabulary of a speaker or a text; and semantics, the meaning system — denotation, connotation, synonymy, antonymy. Grammar describes the systematic principles holding at these levels, and the paper's stated objective is that grammar be handled not in isolation but by creatively integrating it with text.

Grammar taught from a text differs in kind from grammar taught as a list of rules: the teacher asks where the subject is, which word does the work of the verb, and what would change if quietly were not there. The child then meets the rule as a fact about this text rather than as an arbitrary prohibition.

2.5 Morphology, Phonics and Phonology

Where 2.4 asked how a language is sounded, 2.5 asks how it is built. The unit of study is the morpheme — the smallest unit of a language that carries meaning. A morpheme may stand alone as a whole word, in which case it is a free morpheme (book, water, child, run). A morpheme that cannot stand alone but only attaches to another to make a new word or a changed meaning is a bound morpheme (-s, -ed, un-, -ly, re-, -able), and the bound morphemes that attach to the front or the end of a word are called affixes.

Three operations account for most of the vocabulary an eight-year-old meets. Affixation — adding a bound morpheme: un-+happy → unhappy, teach+-er → teacher. Compounding — joining free morphemes: book+shelf, sun+flower. Conversion — one word class used for another with no added affix: a stone, to stone, stoned.

Add derivation, which changes a word's class or lexical meaning and is productive (the source of -er, -ist, -tion, -ness, -ful, -less, -ly), and inflection, which changes grammatical form — number, tense, comparison — without changing the class (the source of -s, -es, -ed, -ing, -er, -est and the irregular forms). A child with a handful of root words can then produce a very large number of words, which is the linguistic fact behind teaching word families: knowing the root play and the two patterns is enough to build playful, played and playground unassisted.

It is clear why the syllabus brings phonics back in 2.5: the child reaches morphology through phonics. Having learned to take a printed word apart into its sounds, the child notices that the sounds group into chunks and that some chunks recur meaningfully across many words — that play sits inside playful, played and playground alike. Sound awareness gives the chunking; morphological awareness gives the meaning of the chunk.

Together they produce the ability to read unfamiliar words, which is what comprehension is made of. This is also why reading is not taught by a single method: systematic and explicit in the sound–letter relationship, combined with extensive use of meaningful and connected text. A child given only phonics reads aloud correctly and understands nothing; a child given only whole-word exposure guesses from context and cannot read an unfamiliar word.

Unit 3Language Diversity and Multilingualism

Unit 3 brings the paper home. The constitutional framework of language in India, the multilingual classroom, the language map of Assam, and the three-language formula are all, in different ways, statements about what a school must do with the diversity in front of it. The content is both descriptive and prescriptive, and it is examinable at a high level of detail.

3.1 Constitutional Provisions about Language

Language occupies a place in the Constitution that no other subject occupies in quite the same way. It is present in the Fundamental Rights, in the Directive Principles, and in the Eighth Schedule. The provisions are collected in Part XVII of the Constitution, devoted to "Languages", with a Chapter I of general provisions (Articles 343, 343A, 344, 345, 346, 347, 350, 350A, 350B) and a Chapter II on official language (Article 351); Article 352 stands separately for special provision for English.

Article 350A is a similar duty in a different form, concerning specifically the mother tongue at the primary stage and of those who are not able to study in the mother tongue. Finally, the Eighth Schedule is a list of recognised languages; it is neither an official-language list nor an exhaustive list of the languages spoken in India.

The Eighth Schedule

The Eighth Schedule is the list of languages recognised by the Constitution. It originally contained 14 languages. The Twenty-first Amendment Act, 1967 added Sindhi, making the number 15. The Fifty-fifth Amendment Act, 1987 added Konkani, Manipuri and Nepali; the implementation of that addition was held up by litigation, and the Seventy-first Amendment Act, 1992 gave the addition retrospective effect. The present list therefore contains 22 languages, including Assamese (Bamar).

3.2 Multilingualism and the Multilingual Classroom

Multilingualism is the condition in which more than one language is in use in a community or setting, and in which more than one is needed for full participation.

Key Idea

Multilingualism is not a problem to be solved; it is a resource to be used. A single classroom in Assam may contain Assamese, Bodo, Mising, Hindi, Bengali and a tea-garden or migrant community language in one morning.

The multilingual classroom reflects this condition, and it is more accurately described as multilingual than as multicultural: here the languages are the medium through which culture, relationships and learning are conducted. Three features distinguish it from an ordinary classroom that happens to contain children of several languages. Languages are additive, not remedial — a language the child brings adds to the school's repertoire rather than correcting a deficit, and there is no "weak" and "strong" language, only different ones.

Functions are distributed — one language may carry explanation, another instruction, another affection or play, and the child learns to shift while the school makes the shift possible. All the languages are used deliberately — the classroom plans for the languages present and uses them purposefully for content, comparison and enrichment.

Multilingualism also brings a real pedagogical gain that is often overlooked. Having more than one language means more than one set of comparisons, and comparison is the origin of grammar. A child who knows that the same idea is expressed by two different word orders, or two systems of marking tense, or two scripts, acquires consciousness about language itself — metalinguistic awareness — far sooner than a monolingual peer. Grammar becomes visible as something languages do differently, not as a set of arbitrary rules one language happens to impose.

Two conditions must be stated as necessary. There must be a shared language for the classroom's common purposes, or no lesson can proceed; multilingualism does not mean that everything may be done in any language at any time. And the children who do not yet know the school language need it explained with patience and time. A classroom that is multilingual but has abandoned the goal of teaching the school language has not solved anything.

3.3 Language Diversity in the Context of Assam

Assam is a particularly instructive case, because a small state with a long history of trade, migration and settlement contains a remarkable density of languages. Assamese (Asamiya Bhasa, also called Bamar) is the official language of the State; it is written in the Baamuniya (Bamuniya), a script of the Brahmic family, and is one of the 22 languages of the Eighth Schedule. The Assam Official Language Act, 1963 established Assamese as the sole official language of the State. Assamese is spoken over a wide area as a first and a second language and functions as the lingua franca of the State — which is why the official-language question in Assam has never been simple.

Alongside and within the Assamese-speaking population, Assam is home to a very large number of tribal and linguistic communities. Among the better known are Bodo (itself in the Eighth Schedule, with its own script), Mising, Karbi, Dimasa, Hmar, Zakhai, Khasi, Garo, Rabha, Tiwa, Kachari, Son and Deodhai (Dhodar), together with tribal communities in the hill districts of Dima Hasao and Karbi Anglong.

The tea gardens brought in workers from many parts of eastern and central India, and with them languages such as Sadri or Mundari, along with Nepali-speaking groups; Bengali and Hindi are widely used in commerce and in urban conversation. The result is that a single district, and in some cases a single school compound, may contain half a dozen mother tongues.

Language questions in Assam are also questions of public policy. The State has a Language Policy for Assam with a long history of revision, and a significant constitutional dimension: Article 343A is the provision under which the State adopts Assamese as its own official language; Article 347 obliges the State to endeavour to promote the Eighth Schedule languages; and Article 371 permits special provisions for Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Nagaland, on which footing measures for the Assamese language and for the languages of the north-eastern region are understood to rest.

The policy has been the subject of judicial consideration and continues to be a matter of active adjustment rather than settled law. State the constitutional position precisely, state that a Language Policy for Assam exists and has been revised from time to time, and avoid asserting any specific statistic or judicial holding that cannot be defended.

What this means for a D.El.Ed teacher is concrete. The children in one class will not all have Assamese as their mother tongue: a Bodo-speaking, a Mising-speaking and a Hindi-speaking child sitting in an Assamese-medium Class II are all learning a second or third language at the same time as they are learning to read. The school has a constitutional and a pedagogical obligation towards each of them, and an interest in their languages, because those languages are resources — they carry the children's own concepts, their folk literature, and community knowledge the textbook does not contain.

3.4 Multilingualism as a Resource; Role of the Teacher

Multilingualism becomes an advantage only if it is used. Left alone it is a set of languages sitting in a room — which is why the teacher's role shares a syllabus point with multilingualism as a resource. For content, a child can explain a concept first in the language in which they know it best, and the teacher bridges to the school language afterwards; this planned, principled movement is translanguaging, not random code-switching. For vocabulary, children bring their own words for a plant, a bird, a food, a tool, and the class holds all of them beside the textbook word.

For grammar, two languages side by side reveal the rules of both. For literature, a folk tale in a home language is a text to be analysed, not a distraction. For assessment, a child who cannot yet write in the school language can dictate and still be credited for the content. For social relations, structured pair work across language groups stops the classroom from mirroring the linguistic map of the neighbourhood.

This is also why the practicum project on the languages of the neighbourhood is serious work: it asks the student-teacher to find out what the children actually speak, which is the necessary first step to teaching them well.

3.5 The Three-Language Formula (R-1, R-2, R-3) and Its Importance

The three-language formula was recommended by the Education Commission (Kothari Commission), 1964–66, and adopted as a national policy in the National Policy on Education, 1968. It is a compromise, and an ingenious one: it acknowledges the child's mother tongue, the linguistic majority of the State, and the need for a language of wider communication, and it tries to teach all three in a graded sequence so that no one is left out.

The formula is conventionally written as R-1, R-2, R-3, and two common readings should both be known. In the widely used school rendering, R-1 is the regional language of the State, R-2 is the mother tongue or local language of the child, and R-3 is a modern European or foreign language, in India ordinarily English.

The Kothari Commission's own formulation for the secondary stage also spoke of learning English (or another foreign language), the mother tongue or local language and the regional language, with the regional language and the mother tongue beginning at the primary stage and the foreign language introduced later. A candidate who gives only the labels without saying which language each stands for in a given state will be marked incomplete; the labels are a shorthand, and the content of each slot is the real answer.

Table 3.5 — The three-language formula in an Assamese-medium school
SlotGeneral meaningIn an Assamese-medium school in AssamIntroduced / emphasised at

R-2Mother tongue or local languageThe child's own home language — Bodo, Mising, Karbi, Hmar, Sadri/Mundari, Bengali, or anotherFrom the beginning, at home and, where possible, in school

It is worth being explicit that in an Assamese-medium school the child's home language is R-2, not R-1, and that the two frequently coincide — when the child is Assamese-speaking at home, the school simply reinforces it, which is a very different situation from the Bodo- or Mising-speaking child for whom the school language is a second language from the first day.

The importance of the formula lies in what it achieves and in what it tries to avoid. It avoids reducing the child to a single language, which destroys the mother tongue; reducing the child to the majority language alone, which produces a schooling system in which a large part of the country learns slowly; and reducing the child to the foreign language alone, which produces an elite unable to communicate with the society it governs. Its weaknesses are the mirror image of its strengths: a rigid interpretation can overload a young child with three languages at once, and can allow the foreign language to be used to make the poor feel backward.

NEP 2020 has moved the position, and an answer that gives only the 1968 formulation is out of date. NEP 2020 recommends multilingualism generally, and specifically that the medium of instruction will be the home language / mother tongue or the regional language until at least Grade 5, but preferably till Grade 8. It also sets out the 5+3+3+4 structure — Foundational Stage (three years pre-primary plus Grades 1–2), Preparatory (Grades 3–5), Middle (Grades 6–8), Secondary (Grades 9–12) — within which the mother tongue carries the child through Foundational and Preparatory while other languages are progressively introduced. NCF-SE 2023 builds on this, treating language as the medium of the whole curriculum and the Vocabulary of Indian Languages as a national resource.

Unit 4Language across the Curriculum

Unit 4 is the shortest unit by marks and the most practical. It asks what language is for inside a school: where it stands in the curriculum, what the difference is between teaching language and learning through it, what work language does in and around the classroom, and what two kinds of proficiency a child needs — the everyday conversational kind and the academic kind.

4.1 Place of Language in the Curriculum

Language occupies three distinguishable places in the curriculum, and confusing them is the most common error in discussions of the subject. It is, first, a subject: Assamese (or English) has its own periods, its own textbook, its own syllabus, its own examination, and its own body of knowledge — literature, grammar, composition, comprehension.

It is, secondly, a medium: the vehicle through which every other subject is taught, every lesson is explained, every question is asked and every answer given, and through which the child demonstrates what has been learnt in history, science, mathematics or art. It is, thirdly, a resource and a cross-curricular competence: the set of abilities — listening, speaking, reading, writing, reasoning about language, and using language to learn — which every other subject both assumes and develops.

The tension between these three places is real and it is the ordinary experience of every school. Time given to "language" is time apparently taken from mathematics; but a child who cannot read the question cannot do the mathematics, and a child who cannot write the answer cannot show that the mathematics was done. The resolution is not to choose but to plan for the overlap: build the language demands of each subject into its planning, and build the subject content into the language period. NCF-SE 2023 and NEP 2020 both move in this direction, treating language not as one subject among many but as the medium of the whole curriculum, with the home language or regional language carrying the child as far as possible.

4.2 Language Learning and Learning through Language

These are two different activities that the word "language" conceals, and a primary teacher must be able to say which one a lesson is doing. Learning about language treats language as the content: naming its parts, comparing constructions, applying a rule, revising, summarising, translating. The child is doing something to language. Learning through language treats it as the tool of another purpose: learning about plants, or the water cycle, or the difference between 46 and 39, with language used to think, organise, discuss and write. Most of school happens in this second mode and little attention is paid to it, which is why a child can be taught minute details of grammar and still be unable to explain a process in a coherent paragraph.

A subject period often consists of the second mode with none of the first, and the two must be connected deliberately. Three moves do this well. Planning the language of the subject: for a lesson on the life cycle of a butterfly, decide in advance that the children will label a diagram, write four steps in order using first, then, next, finally, and compare their sequence with a partner.

Those are language demands, and they were chosen, not inherited. Using subject content as language material: the science text is also a text, and its headings, sequence and cause-and-effect markers are grammar taught in context. Reading across subjects: a history paragraph is comprehension work, a mathematical word problem is inference work. The subject is the vehicle and the subject matter at once.

4.3 Functions of Language Inside and Outside the Classroom and School

Language in a school does far more than carry the timetable. It performs identifiable functions, and a teacher who can name them can also plan for them. Inside the classroom the principal functions are: instructional (explaining, describing, defining, giving directions, demonstrating, summarising); disciplinary (praising, warning, rewarding, correcting behaviour); affective and motivational (encouraging, greeting, expressing interest and approval); evaluative (judging work, giving feedback); social and organisational (managing group work, sharing, negotiating, resolving conflict); and record-keeping and administrative (register, attendance, marking, reporting to parents).

Outside the classroom and beyond the school the functions are equally varied: at play and on the street, in the family and among neighbours, in the market and on the phone, in worship and in festival, in folk song and story, in the newspaper and on the radio, in the classroom of the next stage, and in the wider world of work and further study.

Two observations follow. The first is that the classroom is a high-status register: formal, complete, impersonal, planned — and children spend hours in it. The school therefore has a duty to supply the other registers too, through storytelling, songs, games, discussion, role play and the reading of literature, or the children leave fluent in one register only. The second is that the languages used outside are exactly the languages the child is assessed inside in. A school that forbids Bodo or Mising on the playground while requiring Assamese in the classroom has produced a child who is silent in one and fluent in the other.

The teaching implication is a modest one and a useful one: to teach a function, give children an occasion to perform it. A child will not learn the language of negotiation by being told about it; a child will learn it by being given a real disagreement about the distribution of a task in a group, and being made to resolve it. The literary activities of the practicum — debate, extempore speech, storytelling, role play — are the occasions on which the affective, argumentative and presentational functions of language are actually practised.

4.4 Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills and Cognitive and Academic Language Proficiency

These two terms, usually written BICS and CALP, come from the work of Jim Cummins. BICS — Basic Interpersonal Communication Skills — is the everyday, face-to-face language of survival and social life: greeting, asking for something, thanking, apologising, describing, arguing, joking, and understanding what is said to one in ordinary conversation. It is contextual, that is, it is carried by gesture, tone, situation and shared knowledge, and the speaker does not have to be precise.

CALP — Cognitive and Academic Language Proficiency — is the language needed for reading and writing in school and for learning subjects: the language of a textbook, a definition, an argument, an examination answer, a scientific explanation. It is decontextualised, that is, the meaning must be carried by the words themselves, without gesture or shared situation to help, and it is academically demanding.

A child who has BICS can hold a conversation, work in a group, play, and appear entirely at ease — and still be unable to read a word of the textbook. This is not a contradiction; it is a fact about the two kinds of language. A child with BICS in a home language and no CALP in the school language is the normal condition of a child in the first two years of an Assamese-medium school, and also the normal condition of a child learning English as a third language anywhere in India. A child with CALP but little BICS is rarer — often the child who reads well and cannot talk to anyone — and the school can produce it.

Two related ideas from the same body of work are worth knowing, though neither is a syllabus point. The threshold hypothesis holds that there is a level of proficiency — a threshold — below which the cognitive and academic demands of schoolwork cannot be met, however much content knowledge the child has: a child may know perfectly what a seed is and still be unable to read the chapter about it.

The interdependence hypothesis holds that proficiency developed in one language transfers to another, so that a child who has acquired strong academic skills in the mother tongue reaches those skills in the second language faster and more successfully than a child who has not. Both point in the same practical direction: teach the concepts in the language the child has, and the school language will follow.

Remember

BICS is basic interpersonal communication skills — everyday, contextual, spoken, acquired early without teaching. Both are attributed to Jim Cummins. Do not confuse CALP with "calculus" in an exam answer.

Notes on conducting the activities

  • Activity 1 — literary activities (compulsory). Run them with real rules and real audiences: a debate needs a motion, two sides, a chair, a time limit and a vote; an extempore speech needs a topic drawn on the spot; storytelling needs an audience task; role play needs a situation, not a script, so that the children generate the language themselves. Record what was planned, what happened, the language problems observed, and what you would change.
  • Activity 2 — charts and material. Prepare one item you can use yourself — a word wall, picture-word cards, or a chart of the sounds and letters of the language the class reads in — and submit it with a note on the class it was made for.
  • Activity 3 — project on multilingualism. Keep it small: one class of thirty, or a few streets. Ask what language is spoken at home, by whom, in which situations, with which grandparents, and whether any other language is understood. Record anonymously, tabulate, and present the result as a language map using the actual words people used. The finding is that a single class holds several mother tongues.
  • Activity 4 — folk tales, stories and folk songs. Take everything in the mother tongue, not only in the school language, and record it as spoken before writing it down — the written version differs. Note the teller and the occasion. Share the material in the original in class; a folk tale performed in the school language has become a different text.
  • Activity 5 — essays and articles. Choose a language issue you can argue from your own classroom experience — the medium of instruction, the home-language question, the place of the mother tongue. Write at least two and cite the readings used.
  • Activity 6 — case study of a multilingual classroom. Sit through at least three periods. For a sample of children record the language they spoke most fluently, the language in which they were questioned, and the language in which they answered. Compare the three and note when a child went silent and when one suddenly became fluent. The comparison is the finding.
  • Activity 7 — critical analysis of texts. Take one item from each of the four categories — newspaper, children's literature, journal article, textbook page — and for each answer: who is the intended reader, what is the purpose, how is the language suited to it, and what choice of language would serve a different reader better.

Essential Definitions

  • Language — a conventional and systematic symbol system used by a community for exchanging messages, representing thought and creating meaning.
  • Home language / mother tongue (matribhasa) — the language a child acquires first, naturally and informally, in family and community life.
  • Transition — the period during which a child moves between the home and the school language, marked by a temporary reduction in the amount and quality of talk.
  • Phoneme — the smallest unit of sound that can change a word's meaning; contrastive by definition. Phonics — teaching the grapheme–phoneme relationship so that print becomes sound.
  • Morpheme — the smallest unit carrying meaning; free when it can stand alone, bound when it cannot.
  • Metalinguistic awareness — the ability to think about language as a system; the foundation of grammar teaching and of literacy.
  • FLN — Foundational Literacy and Numeracy: the early stage at which every child acquires the basic capabilities of reading, writing, speaking and number.
  • Multilingualism — more than one language in use in a community, with more than one needed for full participation; in this paper, a resource.
  • Mother tongue clause — Article 350A: efforts shall be made to promote the mother tongue at the primary stage, English as an alternative where necessary.
  • Three-language formula — teaching the regional language, the mother tongue or local language, and a foreign language, in a graded sequence.
  • BICS / CALP — the everyday contextual spoken language of social life, and the decontextualised academically demanding language of reading, writing and subject learning.

At the EndCourse Information

Orientation

Language is not only a tool of communication; it is the medium through which nearly all knowledge in a school is received, tested and displayed. A child who arrives at Class I with a rich mother tongue and meets that language in the classroom is learning; a child who arrives with one language and is forced into another is still learning, but at an avoidable cost. Unit 1 treats language as a social and cultural system, Unit 2 turns to the child and to the structure of language, Unit 3 takes up the Indian and specifically Assamese situation, and Unit 4 widens the frame to the curriculum as a whole.

The Language of the Paper

Five of the eight prescribed readings for this paper are in Assamese, so the paper is a bilingual paper in substance. If the question paper is set in Assamese, answer in Assamese and use the Assamese technical vocabulary the readings use. Use the readings in the original: Adhunik Bhasa Bignan Parichaya for the nature of language, Bhasar Twattva Katha for the levels of linguistic structure, and Upa Bhasa Bijnanar Bhumika and Nika Asamiya Bhasa for the Assamese-specific content of Unit 3. A bilingual glossary is given at Table 3.3.

Mode of Transaction

Discussion; pair and group activity; workshop; assignment; project work; questioning and responding; case study; lecture; literary activities. A purely descriptive treatment of grammar is not what is wanted: grammar is to be handled inside text, noticed in the sentences children actually read, not presented as a separate list of rules. The syllabus makes a related point about reading — comprehending an expository or subject-content text draws on quite different strategies from following a narrative, and a teacher who knows the difference teaches reading far better. The practicum is not separate from the teaching of the paper but an activity-based form of it.

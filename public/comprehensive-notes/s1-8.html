Unit 1Introduction to Yoga and History of Yoga

1.1 Meaning, Definitions and Brief History of Yoga

Yoga is a Sanskrit word. It comes from the root yuj, “to yoke, to join”; with the suffix -a the root yields yoga — that which yokes or joins. A practitioner is a yogi or yogini. The imagery is mechanical and agricultural at once: a yoke joins two oxen so that they pull together, and so does yoga join two things that cannot move usefully apart. Which two things is what the definitions debate.

Definition

Patanjali (Yoga Sutras): yoga is citta-vrtti-nirodha — the stilling of the fluctuations of the mind. Citta is the mind and its disturbances, vrtti is fluctuation, nirodha is arrest. This is the classical definition and carries the greatest authority. The widely used phrase “the union of body, mind and soul” is a popular paraphrase, not a translation: yoga means yoking, and Patanjali describes the result as stilling rather than fusion. Quote Patanjali first in an examination answer, then give the paraphrase as a commonly used description. The Mahabharata gives the uniting of the senses and the self, and the Bhagavad Gita (6.4) the stilling of the senses with the mind resting in the Self — both again describe a condition of the mind.

Brief history

The word yoga appears in the Rigveda, among the oldest of the Vedas, in its literal sense of the yoking of a chariot or a team of oxen, with no philosophical content; it appears again in the Atharvaveda. In the Upanishads, and especially in the later yoga-oriented ones, it acquires its philosophical meaning: the Yoga Shikha Upanishad offers an early classification, the Yoga Yajnavalkya, Shandilya and Darshana Upanishads discuss meditative technique, and the Darshana Upanishad in particular gives a systematic treatment of concentration. The Bhagavad Gita, dated by different scholars between the second century BCE and the second century CE, devotes chapters 5, 6, 8 and 12 to the paths of yoga.

The decisive text is the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, which gathered the scattered practices into a systematic eight-limbed discipline. Patanjali is conventionally dated to roughly 200 BCE to 200 CE, but the dating is genuinely disputed — give the range and say so. Ashtanga Yoga in the sense of “the eight-limbed yoga” comes from this text and its later commentaries.

After Patanjali come the classical Hatha works — the Hatha Yoga Pradipika of Svatmarama (fifteenth century), the Gheranda Samhita and the Shiva Samhita — which concentrate on asana, pranayama, mudra, bandha and kriya. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries yoga was revived and spread worldwide: Sri Aurobindo wrote on Raja Yoga, and Dhirananda Saraswati, B. K. S. Iyengar and T. Krishnamacharya brought asana into Indian schools and then to the West. In 2015 the United Nations General Assembly fixed 21 June as the International Day of Yoga.

1.2 Aim and Objectives of Yoga

The aim of yoga is one sentence long: the stilling of the mind, so that the person may act with clarity and steadiness. Everything else — the asana, the breath, the diet, the discipline — exists in order to reach it. Yoga is never a means to some further end such as beauty, strength or marks; strength and health are by-products, and the end is a settled mind.

The objectives are usually stated at four levels, and in the school context those four translate into things a teacher can actually observe in a child.

  1. Physical. To make the body healthy, strong, flexible and steady; to improve posture and the working of the internal organs; to keep the body free of disease. This is the level at which the asanas do their visible work.
  2. Mental. To concentrate well, to think clearly, to remember, to solve problems, to resist the pulls of distraction. This is the level at which dharana, dhyana and disciplined breathing act, and it matters most for a pupil, because learning is a mental activity before it is anything else.
  3. Emotional. To control anger, fear, greed and attachment; to develop patience, tolerance, self-reliance and an even temperament.
  4. Spiritual or moral. To know the Self, to live in harmony with self, society and nature, and to serve. In a secular school this is expressed as self-knowledge and ethical living — the five yamas and five niyamas — without any doctrine being taught.

A fifth, more practical objective belongs in a teacher-education syllabus: yoga is objective knowledge that the body can be kept healthy and the mind trained without medicine, machines or money. Where a health centre may be twenty kilometres away, a ten-minute morning practice that costs nothing and needs no equipment is one of the few health interventions a teacher can guarantee to a whole school.

1.3 Scope of Yoga

Scope means the range of activity the word “yoga” legitimately covers. In this paper the scope is wide in two directions at once: the horizontal, which is the many forms of yoga, and the vertical, which is the depth of each form.

  • Horizontally, yoga covers everything from the single standing posture of Tadasana to the concentrated meditation of samadhi. The syllabus names six main forms — Raja, Hatha, Karma, Jnana, Tantra and Bhakti — treated in Unit 2 — and four modern categories describing what a practice set does for a particular body (supplemental, compensatory, regenerative, power), treated in Unit 4.
  • Vertically, the scope runs from the body to the mind and from the mind to conduct. Patanjali’s eight limbs are the clearest map of it: two limbs govern conduct, one the body, one the breath, one the senses, and three the mind. The five families of practice in Unit 3 — asana, pranayama, mudra, bandha and kriya — with japa and ajapa, all lie within this scope.

Two boundaries should be stated plainly. First, yoga is not medicine. It does not replace diagnosis, medication or surgery, and a teacher has no business telling a parent to stop a child’s prescribed treatment. Second, yoga is not religion, although it has deep roots in Vedic and Vedantic tradition. The school is a secular institution under the Constitution, and yoga is taught as a system of physical and mental health with cultural roots — open to every child regardless of faith, and to children with none.

1.4 Misconceptions About Yoga

Yoga is surrounded in everyday talk by beliefs that are half-true, wholly false, or — in one case that matters a great deal — dangerous. Learn the following as two columns, because an examiner asking “What are the misconceptions about yoga?” wants the misconception and the correction together.

Table 1.2 — Common Misconceptions About Yoga, and the Correct Position

Yoga is only physical exercise, like gym or running.Exercise is a part of it. Yoga also trains breath, attention and conduct; without the mental limbs it is only asana.

A person with a medical condition must be under a doctor’s care, and nobody may advise anyone to stop prescribed medication. This is the one misconception with real danger in it.

1.5 Importance of Yoga in School Education

A school is the one institution that reaches every child of a district, for every year, at the stage when habits are still forming. If a practice costs nothing, needs no equipment, works on an empty classroom floor, improves attention, posture, sleep and mood — and if the school is the only place the child will certainly encounter it — then the school is the only place it belongs. The syllabus gives a graded age guidance, and this is one of the most commonly examined statements in the paper.

Table 1.3 — Age Guidance Given in the Syllabus
AgeWhat the syllabus prescribesWhy
Before 5 yearsLight stretching exercises, light yogic exercise, and balancing activitiesAttention spans are short and the body is still forming; the aim is movement, rhythm and balance, not postures or breath control
From 5 yearsAsana and MeditationThe child can now hold a steady posture and still the mind briefly
From 8 yearsPranayama and other activitiesThe lungs, the attention and the capacity for self-regulation have developed enough for deliberate breath control

Beyond the age guidance, the importance of yoga in a school rests on six claims a future teacher should be able to argue for:

  • Health is the condition of education. A child with a stiff neck, poor posture, shallow breathing and low stamina cannot sit through a lesson.
  • Attention and self-regulation. A ten-minute assembly practice of breath and stillness rehearses exactly the skill a child needs in order to listen, wait and persist, and it does so without a word of instruction.
  • Emotional balance. The school day is full of triggers. Regular practice builds a baseline of steadiness that reduces impulsive reaction.
  • A secular, culturally rooted, cost-free curricular resource. No laboratory, no laboratory fee, no laboratory permission; the mat can be a rug.
  • Inclusivity. Adapted yoga can be offered to children with varied physical capacities, which most competitive school games cannot do.
  • Prevention. “Prevention is better than Cure.” Back pain, postural deformity, obesity and stress-related strain are all cheaper to prevent than to treat.

2.1 Raja Yoga, Ashtanga Yoga and the Eight Limbs

Raja Yoga is the yoga of the king — so called not because kings practise it but because it is the sovereign discipline, the one that commands the others. Its content comes from the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, and its method is called Ashtanga Yoga: ashta = eight, anga = limb. Patanjali divides the whole discipline into eight limbs, arranged as a ladder climbed in order.

Key Idea

Of the eight limbs, the first five are external — bahiranga: they concern life with other people and with one’s own body. The last three, dharana, dhyana and samadhi, are internal — antaranga: they concern the mind alone. Patanjali says that when the internal limbs are practised perfectly the external ones are no longer needed. One sentence carries the whole scheme.

2.2 Hatha Yoga, Its Limbs, and Its Relation to Raja Yoga

Hatha Yoga is the practical, body-oriented system. The word hatha is usually derived from ha (sun) and tha (moon), carrying the sense of the union of the two great currents of the body; in the older usage it also simply means force, firmness, determination. Unlike Raja Yoga, which begins by regulating the mind, Hatha Yoga begins by regulating the body: it purifies it, steadies it, and makes it comfortable enough to sit still long enough to meditate. Its classic texts are the Hatha Yoga Pradipika of Svatmarama (fifteenth century), the Gheranda Samhita and the Shiva Samhita.

Hatha Yoga and Raja Yoga: the relationship

The standard traditional understanding is that Hatha Yoga is the preparatory, body-oriented discipline and Raja Yoga the psychic and meditative discipline for which it prepares. Hatha purifies the body through the shatkarmas, steadies it through asana, regulates the breath through pranayama and holds the energy with bandha and mudra; the effect is a body that is clean, alert and comfortable, and a mind brought to one point. Only then is Raja Yoga — Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi — possible. The traditional image is that Hatha is the ladder and Raja Yoga the summit.

Two qualifications keep this honest. The relationship is a traditional understanding, not a scientific finding; nothing in the classical texts proves Raja Yoga impossible without Hatha. And in modern practice the two are not rivals but overlapping — most contemporary teachers of asana and pranayama are teaching Raja Yoga to a body they first prepared through Hatha. In a school: Hatha Yoga is the preparation, Raja Yoga is the aim, and a child who does ten minutes of asana and pranayama every morning is doing, in miniature, the whole path.

2.3 Karma Yoga, Jnana Yoga, Tantra Yoga and Bhakti Yoga

Alongside the systems organised by limbs there is a second division, older and equally important: by temperament — by the kind of person in whom the search finds its natural shape. The Bhagavad Gita is the chief source: Krishna does not prescribe one path for everybody but says the wise follow whichever suits their own nature. The four classically named paths are Karma Yoga, Jnana Yoga, Bhakti Yoga and Raja Yoga proper. The syllabus adds Tantra Yoga, a later and more esoteric system. Swami Vivekananda made the standard four-fold division: these are not ranks but dispositions, and the wise person follows the one that matches their nature rather than the fashionable one.

Unit 3Concept of Asanas, Pranayama, Mudra, Bandha and Kriya

Asana is the seat you sit on; prana is breath and life; mudra is a seal; bandha is a lock; kriya is an action. Definition

Asana, in the Yoga Sutras, is a state in which the body is steady, comfortable and free from effort — Patanjali’s famous instruction is that asana is the cessation of effort. This is stricter than the school usage, where asana simply means a named posture, and it is worth knowing because it stops a student-teacher from straining. If you are working in an asana you are doing it wrongly; the moment strain appears, you have gone too far.

The order in which the five families operate is the logic of the whole unit. Asana deals with the body. Pranayama deals with the breath, which links body to mind. Mudra and bandha deal with sealing and directing energy. Kriya deals with cleansing the inside. In a session you would clean (kriya), posture (asana), breathe (pranayama), lock and seal (bandha, mudra), and then sit still.

3.2 Asanas by Posture, and Surya Namaskar

The syllabus groups the asanas by the position the body occupies: prone (lying face down), supine (lying face up), standing and sitting, naming twenty-three postures, plus Surya Namaskar. Each is set out below with the meaning of its name, a plain description, its main benefits and one caution. One general instruction covers every posture in this unit: never force an asana. 3.3 Pranayama

Pranayama is the fourth limb of Ashtanga Yoga and the third of Hatha, and it is the direct bridge between body and mind. Prana is the breath and life-force, aya extension or lengthening, yama regulation. Pranayama is therefore the regulation of the breath-force — not merely breathing exercises, but the deliberate purification, lengthening, retention and balancing of the breath.

Every classical pranayama has three stages, and every answer should name them: puraka, the drawing-in of the breath; kumbhaka, the holding, either after inhalation or after exhalation; and rekika, the release. Most of the poses named below are nishala — without retention, without holding — which is exactly what makes them safe for children, for beginners and for anyone with a blood-pressure condition.

3.4 Mudra

A mudra is literally a seal — the seal on a document, or a sign made with the hand. In yoga it is a deliberate arrangement of the hands and fingers, usually with the rest of the body steady in an asana, which concentrates attention and is said to seal or retain the energy of the practice. The hands are used because they are the most immediate instruments of the mind. The syllabus names five, used in three settings — meditation, prayer and concentration.

3.5 Bandha

A bandha is a lock — a deliberate, sustained tightening of a specific muscle group, which is said to close a passage and to direct and retain the flow of energy (prana) along the nadis, the subtle channels said to run through the body. The classical bandhas are three, and they are named for their position rather than for what they do. All three are practised with a mudra and a pranayama, and all three appear in the postures already described: Jalandhara Bandha in Sarvangasana, Ardha Halasana and Setubandhasana; Uddiyana Bandha in the lifting of the abdomen in Dhanurasana and Chakrasana; Mula Bandha in the pelvic floor in Sarvangasana and at the close of a round of asana.

A kriya is a purposeful action. 3.7 Japa and Ajapa

Japa is repetition of a mantra, said or sung, with the mind deliberately held upon it. A mantra is a sound, word or syllable chosen as the object of meditation; the one most commonly used in yoga is Om (also spelled AUM), a sacred sound rather than a word with a meaning. Practising japa is the simplest form of meditation available: you repeat one sound, and every time the mind wanders you bring it back. That act of bringing the mind back is in fact the training.

Ajapa is the same repetition carried on silently and without apparent effort. The prefix a- means “without”, so ajapa is repetition without speech — repetition that has become automatic, so that the breath itself takes on the shape of the mantra. Japa is spoken and attended to; ajapa arises from it as the mind grows quiet, and in the highest stage the repetition subsides into silence altogether.

Safety, Contraindications and Precautions

A contraindication is a condition in which a particular practice should not be performed, because it would aggravate the condition or cause harm. A teacher who cannot name contraindications is not yet qualified to lead a session. The most common serious error in school yoga is not doing an asana wrongly; it is making a student do a posture the student’s body cannot do. The syllabus is explicit that a mentor must not compel student-teachers with reasonable physical problems to do asanas: such a student must be allowed a modified practice, a different asana, or simply to watch and breathe, without loss of marks and without any comment from the class. Modification is not failure, and no one may be shamed for sitting out.

The rules of safe practice

  1. Practise on a clean, dry, non-slip surface — a mat is ideal, a carpet acceptable, a mud floor is not.
  2. Wear comfortable clothing. The syllabus is explicit that students should be advised to wear comfortable clothes for asana: loose, light, non-restrictive, nothing tied tightly at the waist, neck or joints. Remove all jewellery, watches, bangles, hairpins and spectacles.
  3. Practise on an empty stomach — wait at least two hours after a full meal and thirty minutes after a light one. A full stomach is the commonest cause of discomfort in a class demonstration.
  4. Warm up and cool down. Begin with gentle self-massage of the joints, rotation of shoulders, ankles and wrists, and a few rounds of Anulom-Vilom; end with Savasana. Never go straight into a deep posture.
  5. Never force an asana. A posture is a position, not a competition. The rule Patanjali gives is that the practice should be without effort; if there is strain, shake or burning, come out of it and try again more gently.
  6. Never hold a breath beyond capacity. A beginner should practise nishala pranayama — no retention at all.
  7. Never practise while unwell or injured — fever, a bad cold, a stomach upset, a headache, poor sleep or severe fatigue are all reasons to rest.
  8. Take the help of a trained teacher, and modify freely. A posture cannot be correctly learned from a written description alone. Props — a folded blanket, a chair, a wall, a strap — are legitimate parts of yoga, and a person who needs one is doing the posture properly. Never teach a child a posture you have not been taught yourself.
  9. Never give medical advice, and never advise anyone to stop a prescribed medicine. Yoga is complementary to medical care. If a pupil mentions a health condition, the correct action is to inform the parent and the medical practitioner, not to prescribe.

Unit 4General and Therapeutic Benefits of Yoga

Every claim in this unit has to be made carefully, because a syllabus that uses the word therapeutic is not claiming that yoga cures. The general benefits — steadier breathing, better posture, less tension, better sleep, a calmer mind — are well established. The therapeutic benefits are real but narrower, and they are complementary: yoga is something a person does alongside the care given by a doctor, not instead of it.

4.1 Supplemental, Compensatory, Regenerative Yoga and Power Yoga

Health and physical-education syllabi sometimes classify a set of practices not by what it is, but by what it is doing for a particular body. The four categories named in the syllabus belong to this family, and they are the vocabulary a teacher uses when writing a training plan for a particular child or athlete.

It should not be confused with Raja Yoga, from which it shares only a letter, and it is not what a Class III child should be asked to do. For the young, gentle asana and Anulom-Vilom are the right practice.

4.2 Therapeutic Benefits in Lifestyle Diseases

The syllabus names five conditions: anxiety, depression, obesity, hypertension and diabetes. Together these are called lifestyle diseases, because they are largely products of how a person lives — stress, sedentary habit, poor diet, insufficient activity — and that is precisely where yoga is relevant. A lifestyle disease is the one kind of illness for which a daily ten-minute habit can genuinely help, because the cause and the treatment overlap.

4.3 Yoga and Self-Actualisation

Self-actualisation means a person living in accordance with the best of their own nature — aware of what they truly are, choosing it freely and expressing it in action, rather than living according to fear, comparison and habit. In Indian thought the corresponding idea is attaining the Self: Patanjali’s yoga exists so that a person may be free from the restless waves of the mind and see clearly what is real.

The connection runs through the four limbs that concern the mind: pratyahara turns the senses inward, away from the constant demand of the outside world; dharana holds the mind on a single point, the first act of self-direction; dhyana sustains that holding, so that the person acts from a centred place rather than a scattered one; and samadhi is the state in which the person knows, rather than merely believes, who they are. Behind all of it stand the ten ethical disciplines of yama and niyama, without which the inner work becomes self-serving.

International Yoga Day — 21 June

The International Day of Yoga is observed on 21 June every year, the first official United Nations observance having been held in 2015. In September 2014, at the 69th session of the United Nations General Assembly, India proposed an international day for yoga; the draft resolution was adopted unanimously and the Assembly declared 21 June the International Day of Yoga. The date was chosen because it is the day of dakshinayana in the Indian solar calendar — the turning of the sun into the northern hemisphere, the longest day of the year, and the day in the classical tradition best suited to practice.

Since then, 21 June has been marked in India by mass programmes of asana and pranayama in schools, colleges, universities, public offices, army cantonments, villages and colonies, and by International Yoga Seminars. In Assam the day is commonly observed in schools, colleges and community halls, and the district administration and the health department often organise public sessions. If you are asked to conduct one yourself: a mat per participant, a safe open space, a ten-minute sequence, and a clear instruction that anyone with a physical problem may simply sit and breathe.

Essential definitions

Course Objectives

On completion of this course a candidate should be able to:

  1. Explain yoga in its historical setting — its meaning, the classical definitions, its aims and objectives, its scope, the misconceptions attached to it, its importance within school education, and a brief history from the Vedic period to the present.
  2. Describe the various forms of yoga — Raja Yoga and the eight limbs of Ashtanga Yoga, Hatha Yoga and its limbs, the relationship between Hatha and Raja, and the paths of Karma, Jnana, Tantra and Bhakti Yoga.
  3. Demonstrate the practical content of yoga — the concept and correct performance of asanas, pranayamas, mudras, bandhas and kriyas, together with japa and ajapa, applying the principles and precautions of yoga safely to oneself and to children.
  4. Appreciate the general and therapeutic benefits of yoga — the supplemental, compensatory, regenerative and power forms of practice, the therapeutic value of yoga as a complementary practice, the relation of yoga to self-actualisation, and its effects on the six major systems of the human body.

Mode of Transaction

  • Two to three periods a week, combining theory, demonstration and supervised practice.
  • Practice in the TEI in a group, with the mentor observing and recording performance.
  • Field visit, interview or demonstration for Practicum (a); daily assembly practice, a weekly asana session and a half-day demonstration for Practicum (b).
  • Yoga is taught as a secular system of health and mind-culture. No doctrine is taught and no child is required to profess a belief in order to practise.

Assessment Structure

  • External theory examination: 20 marks, four units of five marks each.
  • Internal assessment: 30 marks — assignments 5, practicum and interview 5, unit tests 10, SIP activity 10.
  • Practical programme: 15 marks across Practicum (a) and (b), documented by the mentor’s performance notes.
  • Total: 50 marks for 2 credits at 2–3 periods per week.

About This Paper

This paper sits inside Health and Physical Education, and its place there is not accidental. The syllabus puts it plainly: while the role of education on health has been widely acknowledged, the impact of health on education is often not recognised adequately. We readily accept that a well-taught child learns better; we do not readily accept that a child who sleeps badly, eats poorly, breathes shallowly or sits for six hours a day cannot be taught well at all. The paper also notes that a holistic understanding of yoga education, together with the therapeutic value of yoga practices, helps physiological and mental development.

Yoga therefore arrives in the D.El.Ed not as an imported fitness craze but as a living Indian tradition with textual authority in the Rigveda, the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita and the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali. At the same time the school context is secular: the Constitution of India guarantees freedom of religion, and the school must teach yoga as a secular system of health and mind-culture with Indian roots, not as a religious rite.

Students of any faith, and students with none, practise the same asanas. The evidence supports describing yoga as supportive and worth practising; it does not support describing it as a cure, and these notes do not do that. The guiding motto is “Prevention is better than Cure” — what a child learns to do every morning for ten minutes is cheaper, kinder and more durable than what a doctor must do for him every month.

GuidanceInstruction to Faculty and Mentor

The syllabus sets out explicit instructions to the faculty and mentor of this course. They matter as much to the student-teacher as to the teacher, because they define what good teaching of this subject means and because they tell you exactly what your mentor is watching for. They are reproduced here in substance.

  1. Define the aims and objectives, and the benefits of continuous practice of the pranayamas and asanas. A pupil told to do an exercise without being told why will not do it; one sentence changes the quality of a year of practice.
  2. Discuss the principle of yoga and the precautions to be observed — the body–mind relationship, and the safety rules in Unit 3. Both belong in the first period of the course, not at the end of it.
  3. Advise student-teachers to wear comfortable clothes for asana. Clothing that restricts the waist, neck or knees makes a posture unsafe, and a student-teacher who has not worn suitable clothing cannot teach children what to wear.
  4. Not to compel student-teachers with reasonable physical problems to do asanas. This is the most important sentence in the list. Such a student must be allowed a modified practice, to sit and breathe, or to observe — without loss of marks and without comment from the class.
  5. Note down the performance during the practical sessions for assessment. Attendance is not performance; correct posture of the asana, correct breathing in the pranayama, steady attention in the meditation and safe handling of a modification are what is written down.
  6. Give class assignments — five of the thirty internal marks, and the only component a candidate controls entirely without a third party.

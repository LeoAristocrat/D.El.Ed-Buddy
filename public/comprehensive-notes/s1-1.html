Unit 1 · 15 MarksPerspectives in Child Development

1.1 Growth and Development: Concept and Principles

Growth is measurable, quantitative change in the physical — height, weight, girth, or the number of vocabulary words — expressible in units. Development is qualitative and quantitative change in function, behaviour and personality, in the ways the child organises and uses what they have. The two are not synonyms but supply and use: growth supplies the raw material on which development builds. A child with healthy limbs may never learn to write because nobody taught them; a child with delayed growth may show normal intellectual development in a responsive environment.

Definition

Growth — measurable, quantitative increase in the size, proportion and number of the body’s parts and of vocabulary, in objective units. Development — the progressive series of orderly, coherent changes, qualitative as well as quantitative, by which the child comes to function at progressively higher levels of physical, mental, social and emotional organisation.

The principles of development, most commonly attributed to Edgar Arnold Hurlock, are the working rules that let us predict what a normally developing child should be doing at a given age. Four are usually listed, and the first two are directions of development.

  • Cephalocaudal — head to toe. Control appears first in the muscles of the head and neck, then trunk and shoulders, then arms, then legs and feet. An infant controls the head before the hands, and the hands before the feet.
  • Proximodistal — centre outward. Control moves outwards from the central nervous system and trunk to the extremities: shoulders, then elbow, then wrist, then fingers — the reverse of a chain in which the whole arm moves and only later the individual digits.
  • Development is a continuous process. There is no sudden jump from stage to stage; a skill acquired today rests on what was built yesterday. This is why gaps in the foundation cannot be repaired cheaply later.
  • The rate of development is uneven. Physical growth is rapid in infancy, slows in early childhood and surges again at puberty; language is explosive between two and five; abstract reasoning is a late bloomer. Within one child, one domain may be ahead of another.

Two further principles are widely used. Integration: development in one domain supports the others — secure language lets a child join in with peers, and accepted peers give the confidence to attempt harder cognitive tasks. Individual difference: the sequence of development is broadly common to all children, but the rate, the intensity and the final pattern vary. Development follows a general sequence, not a uniform timetable.

They serve three purposes in a school: to describe what most children can do at a given age, to give a reference against which an individual child is observed, and to guide the planning of activities at the right level — neither so easy that they bore, nor so hard that they produce only failure. A milestone marks an emerging ability, not a mastered one. And milestones are not produced in isolation: sitting up needs trunk control, curiosity and enough floor time.

Never read a late milestone as evidence of poor intelligence, and never treat a milestone list as a checklist for grading children. A child who sits late but is alert, responsive and eager to move has an opportunity problem; a child who sits late, is withdrawn and does not respond requires attention. The teacher’s job is to notice, record and refer.

1.3 Factors Influencing Development

Development is the outcome of two great influences, hereditary (endogenous) and environmental (exogenous), constantly interacting. Heredity supplies the potential, the timetable and the limits; environment supplies the stimulation that makes the most of it. Neither can be substituted for the other. Within heredity three factors matter most: genes, which set the pattern and timing of the growth curve; prenatal and birth factors — maternal nutrition and health, exposure to alcohol or tobacco, difficult delivery, prematurity, low birth weight — which can damage a developmental process before it has begun; and individual genetic endowment, which accounts for differences in the rate and pattern of maturation between children of the same age.

In a rural Assamese-medium classroom, a teacher who notices that many children have never handled a pair of scissors or a skipping rope can convert a factor of deprivation into a factor of development simply by building those into the daily routine.

1.4 Tools for Gathering Data about Development of Children

Everything asserted above was arrived at by systematically watching, asking, measuring and recording children. These six tools are the classical instruments of child study: each describes what a child does, except clinical methods, which diagnose.

1.4.1 Naturalistic Observation

Systematic, unobtrusive watching of a child in a setting where the child behaves naturally — the playground, the classroom, the home, the Anganwadi centre. The observer does not organise the situation; it is allowed to occur and the observer records what happens. Every teacher does this informally, but it becomes a tool only when the watching is planned, focused on a stated behaviour, timed and written down.

  • Decide first what you are looking at and write it down — “the number and kind of social interactions during free play in the first fifteen minutes” — then choose the setting and time, get permission, and position yourself where you can see without being the centre of attention.
  • Keep columns for date, setting and observed behaviour, and keep interpretation strictly separate; mixing the two is the commonest way observation data becomes useless.

1.4.2 Interviews

Conversations held with a purpose, in two main forms. The parent interview elicits history, habits, language used at home, feeding, discipline, health and developmental concerns — the indispensable source of information about a child’s early years that the child cannot supply. The child interview elicits the child’s own account of family, school, likes, fears and worries, and is the only way to hear the child’s perspective on their own life.

  • In use: questions should be short, simple, concrete and open — “what do you like to play?” rather than “what are your recreational preferences?”
  • Sit at the child’s level, do not rush, do not correct, allow silence, and bring a concrete object to talk about.
  • For parents, prepare a schedule of open questions and ask about concrete recent events rather than general impressions.

1.4.3 Case Studies

A detailed, comprehensive, longitudinal study of a single individual, family, group or community, combining many sources of data over time to understand the whole rather than one part. It is the opposite of the controlled experiment: where an experiment isolates one variable by controlling everything else, the case study deliberately gathers everything.

  • In use: build the case from multiple sources — school records, observation, interviews with the child, parents and teacher, medical history, the child’s own drawings and writing, and your reflective journal — and organise it chronologically.
  • Keep three strands visible: what happened, how the child responded, and what the child’s own account was.
  • Because this is the tool the SIP requires, the essential discipline is not to report only the difficulties. Handle it as a professional confidential document, not gossip.

1.4.4 Anecdotal Records

A brief written account of a single, significant incident involving a child, recorded while it is still fresh, in plain descriptive language. Where a case study is a book, an anecdotal record is a postcard.

  • In use: keep a small notebook or index cards and write the record the same day, in five lines — date and time; the setting; what happened, described exactly, including what was said; the child’s words where you can recall them; and a brief interpretation.
  • Write in the past tense of observation, not judgement: “Ranju asked three times to go to the toilet in one period and did not ask again” is data; “Ranju was a nuisance” is not.
  • Over a term, a well-kept record of this kind is the raw material from which patterns emerge, and it is the tool most likely to be used every day by a working teacher.

1.4.5 Reflective Journals

A reflective journal, or diary, records not the child’s behaviour but the observer’s response to it. It asks: what did I see? What did I feel? What did I assume? What did I do? What happened next? What would I do differently? Its purpose is to make the observer’s own thinking visible and therefore examinable, and to correct the most dangerous error in child study — believing that one has seen a child objectively when one has in fact seen a child through one’s own expectations.

  • In use: write regularly, at a fixed time, about a specific incident rather than general impressions.
  • Separate what you observed from what you concluded, physically, by two columns, and end with a commitment to a change in your own practice.
  • It is evidence about the teacher, not the child — invaluable to a reflective practitioner, useless as a diagnosis.

1.4.6 Clinical Methods

Used in a clinical setting by trained professionals to examine an individual child in depth when there is a concern: detailed case history, physical and neurological examination, standardised psychological and educational tests, assessment of vision, hearing and speech, and observation during structured and unstructured tasks. Because they answer “is this child developing normally, and if not, in what way?”, clinical methods are the tools of diagnosis, as distinct from the other five, which are tools of description.

  • In use: a teacher observes, records, refers and follows up; a clinical expert examines, measures and diagnoses. Never attempt to test, screen or diagnose.
  • A child who is not speaking by four, cannot hold a pencil properly at six, or seems not to hear must be referred to the appropriate health or rehabilitation professional.

Unit 2 · 10 MarksPhysical-Motor Development

2.1 Growth and Maturation

Maturation is a different idea from growth and is constantly confused with it. The distinction matters because two children of the same chronological age may be of quite different maturity, and therefore different in what they can do. A teacher who organises activities strictly by age group will repeatedly disadvantage the youngest-maturing child in the class, and a common source of error is to read immaturity as carelessness or low ability.

Definition

Maturation is the genetically determined, largely self-unfolding process by which the physical and neurological systems of the body become capable of allowing new behaviours to appear, independent of and prior to training. Maturation sets the ceiling of what a child is ready to do; practice determines how much of that ceiling is reached.

Three features of the growth curve are worth holding in view. Growth is not linear but sporadic, with rapid and slow phases, and its most dramatic phase is prenatal and the first two years. It follows a roughly sigmoid pattern — slow at first, rapid in infancy, a pause in middle childhood, and a second surge at puberty. And growth in the first three years is so rapid that a child roughly doubles its birth weight in four to six months and roughly triples it by the end of the first year, which is why infant nutrition is a matter of urgency rather than refinement.

2.2 Motor Development: Gross and Fine Motor Development

Motor development is the acquisition of the capacity to move the body and its parts with increasing control, strength, accuracy and coordination. It is the domain in which the environment has the clearest and most immediate effect, because movement must be practised to exist — the developmental argument against an infant kept constantly in an armchair, and against a school that removes play from the timetable on the grounds that it is “not study”.

Gross motor development involves the large muscles and the movement of trunk and limbs: sitting, crawling, standing, walking, running, jumping, skipping, hopping, climbing, throwing, catching, kicking, balancing and swimming. It is acquired earlier, practised spontaneously, and depends chiefly on adequate opportunity, space, safety and freedom. Fine motor development involves the smaller muscles of hands, wrists and fingers and the coordination of hand and eye: grasping, releasing, pincer grasp, threading beads, turning a page, holding a pencil, colouring within the lines, writing, cutting, buttoning, tying a knot. It is slower, more easily delayed by opportunity deprivation, and directly determines school readiness in writing and drawing.

Motor development proceeds in an orderly sequence, remarkably consistent across cultures: sitting before crawling before standing before walking; running before jumping before skipping before hopping; the whole arm before elbow before wrist before fingers. Fine motor is built on the gross motor foundation, and rushing a child to write before the shoulder, elbow and wrist are controlled produces weak, painful grip and a lifelong dislike of writing. Note too that motor coordination, the smooth, timed, balanced use of several muscle groups together as in catching a ball, differs from a motor skill, an isolated capacity such as kicking.

2.3 Role of Parents and Teachers in Providing Opportunities for Physical-Motor Development

A child develops motorically only through practice, and practice requires opportunity, material, time and safety. Those four things are supplied, overwhelmingly, by two adults: the parent and the teacher. Neither changes the child’s genes, and both can determine whether a large part of the child’s motor potential is ever actually used.

Role of the parents

Parents supply the first and most frequent practice environment. The young child’s entire motor world is the home: the floor, the stair, the courtyard, the road, the charpai, the siblings. Parents provide this by allowing movement rather than restricting it — floor time for infants instead of continuous carrying, letting a toddler walk rather than be carried up steps, simple outdoor play space, messy active play even in bad weather, and no battery-operated toy in place of running. Active parents model an active life; parents who consider sport unfashionable or unsafe for girls transmit that view silently and effectively.

Parents also supply the nutrition and health care that are the material of growth, protection from injury — a hazardous staircase, an unguarded well, an open road, a stored pesticide — and the emotional security without which a child will not attempt a difficult movement. A child who expects to be shouted at for spilling will not try to hold a cup steadily.

Role of the teachers

The teacher enters later, but enters with advantages no parent has: regular time, a group, a space designed for many children, expertise, and the authority to change the timetable.

  • Provide space, time and equipment. A safe indoor or covered space, a play period protected in the timetable, and simple equipment — balls, ropes, rings, hoops, skipping ropes, chalk, low benches, climbing frames.
  • Design a developmental sequence. Order the skills from the one the class has to the next, give each adequate practice, let it be practised at the easier level, and only then raise the demand.
  • Gross motor: organised games and free play. A balance beam of two bricks, a low wall to climb, a hoop to jump through, a ball to throw at a target. Free play is not a break from physical education; it is the main occasion on which most children get their gross motor practice.
  • Fine motor: everyday classroom routines. Drawing, colouring, tracing, tearing and cutting paper, threading beads, gripping a brush, clay modelling, blocks, chalk on the slate — as tasks with real materials, not worksheets asking a child to trace shapes it does not yet control.
  • Correct posture and prevent fatigue. Appropriate furniture (desk to elbow, chair to popliteal space) and a limit on continuous sitting are all motor provision. Chronic poor seating is a genuine cause of later postural and spinal problems.
  • Include all children. Motor provision is frequently unequal: the large, clumsy child pushed to the back, the girl kept away from the climbing frame, the child with a disability seated apart. The teacher’s grouping and instruction decide whether every child actually practises.
  • Observe, record and coordinate with parents. The tools of Unit 1 — especially naturalistic observation and anecdotal records — detect a child whose motor development is behind the class and whose difficulty is not laziness; telling a parent in plain language what to provide at home extends the child’s practice hours.
Key Idea

Motor development is the clearest demonstration in the whole paper that development needs opportunity, not merely maturity. The teacher who supplies it needs only a plan, materials, time, and the conviction that a six-year-old should be moving all day.

Unit 3 · 10 MarksCognitive Development

3.1 Meaning and Concept of Cognitive Development

Cognitive development is the progressive change in the child’s thinking. It is the intellectual engine of the whole developmental process, because the child approaches every other domain through it.

Definition

Cognitive development is the sequential and orderly change in the mental processes of a child — perception, attention, memory, thinking, reasoning, problem solving, understanding and symbolisation — by which the child acquires and organises knowledge and forms an increasingly accurate and complex picture of reality.

Three features are essential to hold together. It is both qualitative and quantitative: the child not only accumulates more information but reorganises the way information is held and used. It is constructive: the child is not a passive recipient but an active builder who selects, organises, tests and revises. And it is stage-like rather than smooth in the Piagetian account, the transition from one stage to the next being a change in the structure of thought, not merely an increase in its quantity. A child given more information is not necessarily thinking better: a Class IV child may recite a long list of capitals and be unable to say why they are capitals.

3.2 Stages of Cognitive Development with Reference to Jean Piaget

Jean Piaget (1896–1980), a Swiss psychologist, constructed the most influential theory of cognitive development of the twentieth century. He studied children’s thinking as it actually was — notably in his own three children, over years — and asked how children think, what mistakes they make, and why the mistakes change with age.

  • Children pass through an invariable sequence of qualitatively distinct stages, each with its own structure of thought.
  • Knowledge is constructed by assimilation (fitting new experience into an existing scheme) and accommodation (changing the scheme to fit the experience); the child moves on when equilibration creates enough disequilibrium to reorganise.
  • Two warnings for teachers: a child cannot be taught a stage of thought they have not reached, and a child cannot be held back in a stage — the sequence is invariable, and a ready child will advance whether or not the school is ready.

Stage 1 — Sensorimotor (0 to 2 years)

In the sensorimotor stage the infant knows the world through the senses and through movement. Knowledge begins with the mouth and the hands, and everything is learned by doing rather than by being told. Two achievements mark the approach to the end of the stage: object permanence, the understanding that a thing continues to exist when it is out of sight, which is why a child who drops a toy under a blanket will not look for it; and goal-directed action, in which the child coordinates several actions into a plan, such as pushing a chair to reach the shelf.

Classroom implications. The D.El.Ed classroom begins at age six, so these bear on the very young siblings and the Anganwadi stage: learning must be sensory and motor, objects must be allowed to disappear and reappear, language must be attached to the action the child is performing at that moment, and the adult must narrate rather than instruct.

Stage 2 — Pre-operational (2 to 7 years)

The pre-operational child has acquired symbolic function: they can use one thing to stand for another — a piece of wood is a doll, a folded paper is a boat — and this is the foundation of pretend play, of drawing, and eventually of language and all abstract thought. Five characteristics follow from the use of symbols.

  • Egocentrism. The child cannot easily take another person’s point of view and assumes others see the world as they do. This is a normal stage characteristic, not selfishness, and it is why the child who is “wrong” in an argument genuinely cannot see the other side.
  • Intuition rather than logic. Reasoning rests on appearance and the child’s own judgement — “it must be heavier because it is bigger” — without logical verification.
  • No conservation. The child does not understand that quantity remains the same when its form changes. Pour the same water from a tall glass into a flat one and the child will insist there is more. Conservation is the classic test of the transition to the next stage.
  • Centration. The child focuses on one aspect, usually length or height, and ignores the others.
  • Irreversibility. The child cannot mentally undo an action, so the pre-operational child who has poured the water cannot mentally pour it back.

Classroom implications. The pre-operational period is the years a D.El.Ed teacher teaches in, and Piaget’s conclusion — that the child of this age learns best through activity, discovery and play rather than verbal instruction — shaped twentieth-century early childhood education completely. In practice: use concrete materials rather than abstract rules; demonstrate the conservation experiment rather than explaining it; allow dramatic and pretend play as a legitimate learning mode; accept the child’s point of view without ridiculing it when correcting reasoning.

Stage 3 — Concrete operational (7 to 11 years)

The child can now perform logical operations, but only on concrete material that is present and visible. Four operations characterise the stage: conservation, understanding that weight, volume, mass and number do not change when the form changes; reversibility, the ability to mentally undo an action, which makes work checkable, revisable and self-correcting; classification, grouping things by common properties and understanding that a thing belongs to several classes at once; and seriation, arranging objects in order along a quantitative dimension such as size or weight.

The child is still substantially egocentric, which is why the peer group and rule-governed competitive games are so powerful at this age, but can now take another’s view. This stage is the elementary school. Classroom implications: because operations work on the concrete, the elementary curriculum should be built on concrete material — objects, blocks, apparatus, models, pictures, the actual objects being counted, weighed and measured. A Class III child should do the conservation experiment and then be told about it; a Class V child should sort and arrange real objects before being asked to write the rule. Checking, reversing, classifying and ordering are the foundation of arithmetic, reading comprehension, science observation and organisation.

Stage 4 — Formal operational (11 years onwards)

From about eleven years, and inconsistently until the mid-teens, thinking becomes abstract and hypothetical. The adolescent can reason about possibilities rather than only facts, can formulate and systematically test hypotheses, can consider a system as a whole, can detect inconsistency in an argument, and can think about thinking itself. This is the stage in which mathematics, science, formal logic, literature and abstract moral argument become genuinely accessible.

Classroom implications. The secondary teacher’s task shifts from giving information to creating situations in which hypotheses can be formed and tested — open-ended problems, projects, debates, investigations and ethical dilemmas suit this stage; recitation of rules and reproduction of formulae does not. Many children leave the elementary school still functioning concretely in at least some areas, so provide concrete scaffolding rather than assume the stage has arrived.

3.3 Factors Influencing Cognitive Development

As in 1.3, the factors fall into hereditary and environmental groups, but the cognitive domain is particularly sensitive to one feature of the environment: the quality and quantity of language, and the stimulation the child receives.

  1. Genetic and biological endowment. The genes determine the size and structure of the brain, its efficiency, the timing of neural development and the individual pattern of intelligence. Prenatal nutrition, maternal illness, birth complications and later health affect cognitive development directly, and damage in the prenatal period and first two years is hardest to compensate for.
  2. Language and communication environment. Cognitive development rides on language. The amount and quality of talk a child hears, whether adults ask questions and answer them, whether the child is read to, and whether conversation is in a language the child understands well all shape vocabulary, memory, reasoning and the ability to think in words. The gap in vocabulary at school entry is one of the best-known predictors of later attainment.
  3. Stimulation, experience and opportunity. Books and learning materials, guided rather than merely allowed play, interaction with adults and older children, exposure to varied situations, and encouragement to ask questions, guess, try, fail and try again.
  4. Family, school and socio-economic influences. Parental expectations, home stability, the parent’s own education and attitude to schooling; the teacher’s approach, class size, quality of instruction and the assessment system; and the material conditions reflected in nutrition, health, housing and a quiet place to study. The child’s own characteristics — motivation, effort, self-concept, attention — form a fifth group and interact with all the others.

Almost every factor here passes through a moment when an adult talks to a child. Asking an open question and waiting, reading aloud for fifteen minutes a day, putting a real object into a child’s hands instead of only its name on the board, and accepting the child’s attempt even when the reasoning is wrong — these are cognitive interventions that cost nothing.

Unit 4 · 15 MarksSocial, Emotional and Language Development

These three developments are usually taught separately but occur together and constantly influence each other. A child who cannot talk cannot make friends; a child rejected by friends learns helplessness; a child who feels anxious cannot concentrate, and a child who cannot concentrate falls behind and is rejected. Read the three sub-sections as one argument.

4.1 Social Development

4.1.1 Meaning and Nature of Social Development

Social development is the process by which a child learns to become a member of a group, to understand the expectations of that group, to form relationships, to cooperate and compete, to resolve conflict, and to see the self as others see it. It moves the child, gradually and over some fifteen years, from a wholly self-contained organism to a person who can take a place in a family, a friendship, a classroom, a community and a nation.

Definition

Social development is the progressive process by which a child acquires the knowledge, skills, attitudes and values needed to interact effectively and happily with other people, beginning with the family and extending to the wider community, and through which the child forms a sense of self as a social being.

Social development has two interlocking aspects. The first is social performance — observable behaviour with others: sharing, helping, leading, following, negotiating, excluding, teasing, fighting. The second is social understanding — the growing capacity to understand other people’s feelings, intentions and points of view, to predict what they will do, and to understand the rules governing their interaction. A child may be perfectly well behaved and understand nobody, which is why the two must be assessed separately. Three features deserve naming: it is learned, the first and largest lessons coming from the family; it is reciprocal, accomplished between people rather than done to the child; and it is inevitably mixed with emotion, since social experience is the main source of emotional learning.

4.1.3 Factors Influencing Social Development: Home, School, Peer and Community

  • Home. The first and most enduring social environment. It sets the child’s basic attitude to other people — whether the world is a place to be trusted or one to be defended against — and supplies attachment, the model of communication, the treatment of feelings, and the earliest experience of conflict and its resolution. The child’s temperament also matters: an infant who is difficult to soothe may be handled less patiently and so socialised less richly.
  • School. The child’s first large, structured, non-family social system, and two things at once — a source of social opportunity (the class group, games, teams, shared work, and above all the peer group that forms inside the class), and a source of social learning as a system (regularity, authority, rules, fairness, waiting, taking a turn, coping with boredom, and the fact that a person’s standing does not depend only on their family). The teacher’s own conduct is part of the social curriculum.
  • Peer group. From about six, and certainly from about ten, the peer group becomes a powerful agent: the child’s first chosen social group, where they are evaluated by people who have nothing to offer except friendship. Peer groups sort themselves into friendships, gender groups, cliques and outcasts, and membership confers status. A child’s peer reputation may predict their school life better than their home circumstances, and a peer group can be a force for the good (cooperation, courage, generosity) or for the bad (exclusion, teasing, pressure, risk-taking).
  • Community. The neighbourhood, the village, religious and social institutions, the Anganwadi and health centre, the market, the playfield and the media widen the child’s social world beyond the school, supplying role models, a wider range of social norms, and contact with people of other ages, castes, religions, tribes, occupations and abilities. Community also carries a specific risk in the Assamese context: the custom, in some communities, of treating the child as a worker or a responsibility rather than as a social being with rights, which removes the child from play, from school and from ordinary peer life altogether.

4.2 Emotional Development

4.2.1 Meaning of Emotional Development

Emotion is the child’s first language and the fastest means of communication: an infant who cannot speak communicates hunger, fear, anger, joy and distress entirely through the face, the body and the cry.

Definition

Emotional development is the progressive process by which a child acquires the capacity to experience, recognise, express and control emotions, to form an increasingly differentiated emotional repertoire, to become attached to people, and to share and understand the emotional states of others, so that a regulated, socially responsive and self-aware person is gradually formed.

4.2.2 Functions of Emotion

Emotions are not nuisances to be suppressed. They serve at least seven identifiable functions in the life of a child.

  1. Signal. Emotion signals to others what a child needs and how they are. The distress that brings a caregiver running is the child’s first communication, and the whole system of attachment is built on it.
  2. Motivating. Emotion energises and directs behaviour. Interest and joy sustain attention; fear and anger produce avoidance or aggression.
  3. Communicating. Even before language, emotion is a message, and much of the emotional content of speech — tone, emphasis, expression — is read correctly by the listener before the words are.
  4. Adaptive and protective. Fear and anxiety have evolved to protect the child from danger. Removing the capacity for fear is not a developmental achievement.
  5. Social bonding. Shared emotion is the basis of attachment, sympathy, empathy and love. Children bond to caregivers, and friends are held together by common feeling rather than shared tasks.
  6. Evaluating and decision-making. Emotion is part of the basis on which a person judges a situation as good or bad, safe or threatening, fair or unjust. Guilt and pride are the emotional forms of moral self-assessment.
  7. Self-knowledge and stress. Emotion carries the child’s sense of self-worth, and sustained emotional strain is expressed psychosomatically — in sleep disturbance, stomach aches, withdrawal, aggression or school refusal. The body of a distressed child often speaks before the child does.

4.2.3 Development of Emotion across Stages

Emotion develops along two lines at once: the repertoire grows, the child passing from a few basic emotions to a large and finely differentiated range; and control improves, the child passing from immediate, unmodulated, all-or-nothing expression to graded, delayed, socially managed expression.

4.2.4 Factors Influencing Emotional Development

  • Temperament — the easy, the difficult and the slow-to-warm, with which every infant is born, setting the initial intensity, regularity and adaptiveness of emotional response.
  • Quality of the attachment relationship in infancy, which becomes the child’s working model of all later close relationships.
  • Parenting style — a warm, responsive and reasonably firm style supports healthy emotional development; an anxious or intrusive style produces inhibition; a harsh or rejecting style produces insecurity and, later, the aggressive or withdrawn patterns described in Unit 5.
  • Family stability — separation, loss, violence and chronic conflict in the home are powerfully corrosive to a child’s emotional life.
  • Peer group and school — model for emotional expression, rules for managing feeling, and a source of success or of repeated failure.
  • Self-concept and adult feedback — a child repeatedly told they are careless will become a child who is careless.

4.3.2 Factors Influencing Language Development

  • Quantity and quality of talk in the home — the strongest predictor. Children who hear many words in varied and responsive conversation learn more words; the amount of adult talk directed at the child, rather than television, is what matters.
  • Responsiveness — language develops through turn-taking. When an adult answers a child’s sound or word, the child learns that language produces results; when it is ignored, or the child is spoken over, the incentive to speak is weakened.
  • Reading aloud and story-telling — the most economical intervention available to a teacher and among the most powerful, giving vocabulary, knowledge of narrative structure, knowledge that print carries meaning, and shared pleasure.
  • Mother tongue and the language of the classroom — a child learns concepts in the language they think in. Where the school language differs from the home language, as in many Assamese schools where a child may speak Bodo, Mising, Karbi, Tiwa or Assamese at home and be taught in Assamese or English, there must be a deliberate bridging strategy.
  • Physical and health factors — hearing is the foundation of spoken language; a child with an unrecognised hearing loss is diagnosed as “late in talking” or as stupid. Nutrition and general health matter too.
  • Stimulation and print exposure — books in the home, printed matter, the child’s own drawing and early scribbling, and a classroom that displays and honours children’s own attempts at writing.
  • Peer and cultural factors — the language of play with peers, songs and games, and the cultural valuing of talk versus silence. A teacher who reads shy silence as a deficit is reading the culture, not the child.
  • School and teacher — the teacher’s own language, the time given for talk, whether a child is ever answered, and the decision to teach in a language the child understands. The most damaging practice is the public correction of every error, which teaches a child that speaking is dangerous.
Common Mistake

Concluding that a child who is quiet in class has limited language. A child who speaks freely in the mother tongue with peers may be reticent in a second language and be quite fluent in the first. The correct question is not “does this child speak?” but “in what language, to whom, about what, and how much?”

Unit 5 · 10 MarksDevelopmental Hazards and Developmental Delays

Units 1 to 4 described what normally happens. This unit describes what can go wrong, along two lines: developmental hazards, conditions and events that threaten to damage development at each stage; and developmental delay, the condition in which a child fails to acquire a skill most children acquire within a given period. An elementary teacher is not a diagnostician but is frequently the first person to see either, and must know what to do.

5.1 Developmental Hazards: Meaning

A developmental hazard is any factor, condition, exposure or event with the potential to disturb, delay or distort the normal course of a child’s physical, mental, social, emotional or language development. The word hazard is used deliberately in preference to trauma, because a hazard need not actually have damaged the child: it is a risk present in the environment, and it becomes damage only when it operates. It operates through three broad routes.

Definition

Developmental hazard is any condition, exposure, event or circumstance, whether physical, biological, psychological or social, which lies in the path of a child’s normal development and has the potential to cause delay, distortion or damage in one or more of the developmental domains.

  • Direct injury or damage to the developing brain or body: trauma, poisoning, infection, severe malnutrition, untreated illness, exposure to heat, cold, fire or flood.
  • Deprivation of what development requires: nutrition, medical care, stimulation, language, emotional care, education, safety, shelter.
  • Psychological and social disruption of the child’s attachments, sense of safety and pattern of daily life: separation, loss, violence in the home, displacement, abuse, neglect, war, removal from a familiar language and place.

The concept of critical and sensitive periods is central. Some windows are critical, in which damage occurring at that time is severe and difficult to reverse; others are merely sensitive, in which the effect is real but can be compensated later. This is why prenatal care and the first two years attract so much attention: they are the periods in which the cost of damage is highest.

5.2 Developmental Delay: Meaning, Causes, Symptoms and Types

5.2.1 Meaning and Concept

Definition

Developmental delay is the persistent failure of a child to attain, within the expected period, a developmental milestone that the majority of children reach by that age, in one or more of the areas of physical-motor, cognitive, language, social, emotional or adaptive development.

It is a descriptive term and not a diagnosis. The delay may be general, affecting most domains, or specific, affecting one or two. The term delay is used rather than retardation or any older term, which is both more accurate and required by current professional usage. A child can therefore be growing normally and still be developmentally delayed — the two are not the same test.

5.2.2 Causes of Developmental Delay

Causes are conventionally grouped as pre-natal, natal and post-natal (before birth, during birth, after birth) or as biogenic, psychogenic and sociogenic. The two classifications overlap, and both should be known.

5.2.3 Symptoms of Developmental Delay

The teacher is not qualified to diagnose but is often the first to observe. Read the symptoms as signals that justify enquiry and referral, not as proof.

  • Physical and motor. Does not attain head control, sitting, standing or walking within the usual period; is markedly flaccid or markedly stiff; uses the two sides of the body asymmetrically; fails to develop a pencil grip; is very short for age or grows very slowly.
  • Cognitive and language. No babble by about twelve months; no meaningful words by about eighteen months; a very small vocabulary at three; no two-word combinations by two years; does not respond to a name; does not point to show what they want; does not imitate; loss of skills already acquired at any age — a serious and often overlooked sign.
  • Social and emotional. Does not smile; shows no preference for familiar faces; is markedly passive or markedly active without purpose; shows no interest in other children; does not separate easily from the primary caregiver; is persistently distressed, withdrawn, or aggressive without cause.
  • Sensory. Does not turn towards a sound; does not look at the teacher’s face; does not respond to being named; covers one ear repeatedly; unusual flapping, head-banging or hand-wringing; intense fixation on spinning things.
  • Functional and self-care. At an age when most children can be fed, dressed and toileted with help, the child requires assistance in all three; cannot indicate hunger, thirst, pain or the need for the toilet; frequently has unexplained stomach aches and headaches.
Common Mistake

Loss of previously acquired skills is a medical matter and needs immediate referral. A child who could speak and has stopped speaking, or who could walk and has stopped walking, has not “become shy”. Equally, a child who is quiet but alert, responsive and playful, and who simply speaks a different language at home, is not delayed.

Unit 6 · 10 MarksChild Protection and Child Rights

6.1 Child Protection and Child Rights: Meaning and Concept

Meaning of child protection

Child protection refers to the set of activities, laws, services and institutions — governmental and non-governmental — that ensure the survival, development, protection and well-being of every child, especially the most vulnerable, so that no child is subjected to violence, abuse, neglect, exploitation, discrimination, hazardous work or any form of denial of rights. Protection is a deliberate, preventive and remedial activity, and it is the responsibility of the State, of the community, and of every adult who works with children.

The concept rests on three ideas. Protection is universal but targeted: every child has a right to protection, but the State must direct its resources to those at greatest risk. It is preventive as well as remedial: it is better to prevent a child from being trafficked than to rescue one afterwards, and both are required. And it is a continuum of care, from early support in the community through family-based care and, for children who have no family, institutional or alternative care.

Meaning and concept of child rights

A right is not a privilege or a favour granted by an adult; it is an entitlement that belongs to the child simply in being a child. Children were historically treated as the property of the family and as objects of adult charity, with no standing in law. The twentieth century recognised that children have needs and vulnerabilities of their own requiring positive State action, not merely freedom from interference — the difference between a liberty and a welfare approach. These entitlements were then codified in the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child.

Definition

Child rights are the guarantees of protection, care, development, participation, equality and freedom that international law and national law recognise as belonging to every child, irrespective of gender, caste, religion, race, disability, social or economic status, place of birth or any other circumstance.

6.2 Types of Child Abuse

Child abuse is any act, or any failure to act, by a parent, caregiver, relative, institution or stranger that causes, or is likely to cause, harm to a child — physically, sexually, emotionally or psychologically — or that places the child at risk of harm, whether the harm is actual or potential. Two features of that definition matter:

  • It includes failure to act, so neglect — not providing food, care, medical attention, education or protection — is abuse.
  • It includes harm that is only likely, so a threat, a sexual proposition to a child, or a situation of evident risk counts even when nothing has yet happened.

Protectionists commonly use the acronym ASI, for child abuse and sexual exploitation. It is a classification of abuse, not a word, and must never be read as one.

Both the use and the exposure are harmful, and the exposure is far more common Assam has faced this in several forms, including gutka and kharra containing tobacco and areca nut, sold openly near schools. Inhalant and petrol sniffing among street and working children is a recognised problem. Children of parents who use alcohol are exposed from the earliest years
9. Stress, and the risk of suicide Psychological stress arising from abuse, neglect, family breakdown, violence, poverty, harassment, failure, pressure, loss or displacement, and its most extreme expression in the child who thinks about or attempts self-harm. Stress is the pathway through which most other forms of abuse do their damage Risk is linked to poverty, parental substance use, domestic violence, displacement caused by floods and erosion, school bullying, and pressure on children already struggling. The response is one known adult to talk to, and rapid access to counselling — never punishment for a disclosure

Indicators of Abuse and the Role of the Teacher

A teacher is not an investigator and cannot prove anything. What the teacher can do is notice, record, consult the designated child protection functionary, and report. The indicators below are observation prompts, not a diagnostic checklist; no single one means anything on its own, and it is the pattern, the persistence and the mismatch between the injury and the explanation that matter.

Table 6.4 — Indicators of Abuse, by Category

Four rules govern a teacher’s response, and they are what an examination answer is usually looking for.

  • Notice and record. Write down the date, what was seen, and the child’s own words, in neutral language — the same day.
  • Refer, without delay. Report to the school’s child protection functionary, to the Child Welfare Committee, or to the child helpline 1098, which anyone may call. Under POCSO the offence is reportable by whoever knows of it.
  • Never investigate, never promise secrecy, never doubt the child. Questioning a child turns a disclosure into an interrogation, and a promise of secrecy silences the next one.
  • Never return the child to the risk. Keep the child in school while the process runs. Sending a child back to the person responsible for the harm is the one outcome that must be avoided.

  • Child helpline 1098 — a telephone helpline for children in distress, available to anyone, including a teacher.
  • Special Court and Special Public Prosecutor — created under POCSO for the trial of offences against children.
  • The school child protection functionary — the person in the school who receives a child’s disclosure and takes it forward; the point of contact with the CWC.

Four field tasks, moving from the printed word, to the screen, to the home, to the school — together they build the observational habit that the rest of this paper describes.

Newspaper articles on parenting and childhood Over at least a month, collect ten clippings or saved news items from at least three sources, on themes such as discipline and corporal punishment, screen time, nutrition, adolescent behaviour, examination pressure, child labour, child marriage, attendance, immunisation, or public response to a child-protection issue The ten in chronological order, each with headline, date and source, a three-sentence summary and a two-sentence note on the developmental or child-rights principle it illustrates — then a one-page synthesis.   • Task 3. Do not turn the interview into an interrogation about faults, or write in a tone of moral superiority. The most damaging error is to record parents’ answers in a form that makes them look foolish for practices entirely understandable given their circumstances.
  • Task 4. Height-for-age indicates stunting (chronic undernutrition or repeated illness); weight-for-height indicates wasting (acute recent undernutrition). A child can be short but well proportioned, or of normal height and dangerously thin — you need both. Teacher-taken measurements are screening data, not a clinical assessment: they trigger a conversation, a home visit or a referral to the Anganwadi worker or health centre, and nothing more.

SIP · 10 MarksSchool Internship Programme — Case Study of Two Children

The Task — Two Children from Diverse Contexts

The SIP component of this paper requires a school internship case study of two children from diverse contexts. The syllabus indicates the kinds of diversity expected: children from different socio-economic backgrounds, differently abled children, and street and slum children. The two must be genuinely different, because the purpose is comparison — the same developmental lens applied to two lives shaped in opposite ways. Any pair is acceptable provided the difference is real, ethically obtained, and not sensational.

How to Carry Out the Study

  1. Permission and consent. Obtain the school’s written permission and the parent’s informed consent for each child, and explain what you will do with the information. A case study of a real child is a confidential professional document, not a presentation piece.
  2. Multi-source data. Use at least four of the six tools of section 1.4 — naturalistic observation, interviews with parent and child, a case study of each child, anecdotal records, and your reflective journal.
  3. Cover all domains. For each child record something on physical and motor development, cognitive development, language, social development, emotional development, and any developmental hazard or protection concern. This common structure is what makes the two studies comparable.
  4. Confront the difference honestly. Write the comparative section as a question, not a conclusion: why did these two children, of almost the same age, arrive in this classroom in such different states? The answer will not be a single factor, and saying so is better scholarship than a single-cause explanation.
  5. End with a plan, not a verdict. Three or four concrete actions you would take as a teacher, addressed to the two children and to the class, plus one change you would make to the school’s own practice. A case study that ends only in sadness has not been used.

The hardest and most valuable lesson in the SIP is usually this: the two children are far more alike than their circumstances suggest, and far more different than their test scores suggest. Neither fact cancels the other.

Memory SheetQuick Subject Revision

Essential definitions

Growth
measurable quantitative increase in the size and number of the body’s parts.
Development
progressive qualitative and quantitative change in function and behaviour.
Maturation
the genetically determined unfolding of potential that makes a new behaviour possible without practice.
Milestone
an observable and predictable behaviour marking attainment of a developmental achievement.
Naturalistic observation
watching and recording a child’s behaviour in a natural setting without controlling it.
Case study
a comprehensive, multi-source, longitudinal study of an individual or group.
Reflective journal
a record of the observer’s own thoughts and feelings about what was observed.
Cognitive development
the orderly change in perception, memory, reasoning, understanding and symbolisation.
Developmental hazard
any condition or event with the potential to disturb normal development.
Developmental delay
persistent failure to attain a milestone within the expected period.
Child protection
the totality of laws, services and activities that ensure the survival, development and protection of every child.
Child rights
the entitlements every child holds by virtue of being human.
Child abuse
any act, or failure to act, that causes or is likely to cause harm to a child.

Names, theories and key facts

  • Hurlock — four principles of development: cephalocaudal, proximodistal, continuity, uneven rate.
  • Piaget — four qualitatively distinct stages; assimilation, accommodation, equilibration. Sensorimotor 0–2, pre-operational 2–7, concrete operational 7–11, formal operational 11+. Object permanence ends stage 1; conservation begins in stage 3.
  • UNCRC — child rights as legal entitlements; best interests of the child; participation.
  • Six tools, in order: naturalistic observation, interviews, case studies, anecdotal records, reflective journals, clinical methods. Only clinical methods are diagnostic — the teacher observes, records and refers.
  • Play: solitary, parallel, associative, cooperative, then rule-governed games.
  • Emotion: signals, motivates, communicates, adapts, bonds, evaluates, and carries self-knowledge.
  • Language: crying, cooing, babbling, one-word, two-word, sentences, refinement.
  • Stunting = low height-for-age (chronic). Wasting = low weight-for-height (acute).
  • Loss of previously acquired skills means refer, immediately.
  • Teacher’s duty: notice, record, refer, never investigate, never promise secrecy, never return the child to the risk.

Dates, Acts and schemes

  • UNCRC adopted 20 November 1989; India acceded 11 December 1992; Optional Protocols not ratified.
  • Art. 21A — 86th Amendment 2002 — free and compulsory education, ages 6–14. RTE Act 2009, in force 1 April 2010; section 17 prohibits corporal punishment.
  • Child marriage: girl 18, boy 21. Child labour: 14 general minimum, 18 for hazardous work. POCSO: child = below 18.
  • JJ Act 2000 (amended 2015) · Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006 · POCSO Act 2012 · Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act 1986 (amended 2016) · Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act 2005.
  • Schemes: ICDS (earliest reach) · BBBP, launched 2015 (the girl child) · ICPS (protection response) · Mission Vatsalya, adopted 2021 (rehabilitation).
  • NCPCR is national; SCPCR is State — Assam has one. Do not confuse them. Child helpline: 1098.

What the practicum and SIP require

  • Task 1: ten news items from three sources over a month, plus a one-page synthesis.
  • Task 2: a film on childhood, reflected on in four ways — context, adults, the invisible child, your own response.
  • Task 3: four or five structured parent interviews, tabulated and compared, with what the school should do about it.
  • Task 4: height and weight of one elementary class, analysed as height-for-age and weight-for-height; screening data, not diagnosis.
  • SIP: two children from diverse contexts, four or more tools, all domains covered, ending in an action plan. 10 marks.

Back MatterCourse Information

Course objectives

On completion of this course a candidate should be able to:

  1. review the general conception of the child and of childhood;
  2. understand physical, mental, social and emotional development;
  3. understand the developmental processes of children with diverse abilities in a socio-cultural context;
  4. have hands-on experience of interacting with children; and
  5. understand child rights and protection.

Note the movement from knowledge (i, ii, v) to skill and sensitivity (iii, iv). Objective (iv) is why the practicum is compulsory.

Mode of transaction

At four to five periods a week, this 4-credit paper is transacted through discussion, demonstration, field visit and self-study, supported by the readings at the end of these notes. In practice: lecture–discussion for the conceptual units; worked demonstrations of the developmental milestones in Unit 1; guided practice with observation instruments and interview schedules; and field-based tasks for the practicum components.

Assessment structure

Seventy marks external and thirty marks internal, as set out in the Internal Assessment section above: a unit test (10), participation in a group discussion (5), the written practicum report (5), and the SIP activity (10).

For the MentorFaculty and Mentor Notes

The syllabus does not treat this paper as one that can be carried by exposition alone. It asks of those teaching it:

  • Advance planning. The major content areas — childhood, child protection and child development — are to be planned in advance, not taken up term by term as they are reached.
  • Classroom discussion among the student-teachers for conceptual understanding, rather than presentation to them. This is what makes the group-discussion component of the internal assessment possible at all.
  • Directed reading of news items, articles, e-books, research papers, journals and e-journals on child development.
  • PowerPoint presentations prepared on issues and concerns relating to child development and child protection.
  • An awareness meet on child protection, with resource persons drawn from SCPCR, UNICEF, ICPS, the police department and Childline.

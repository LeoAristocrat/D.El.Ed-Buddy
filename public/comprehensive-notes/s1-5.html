Unit 1 · 5 MarksStatus of English

1.1 Historical Perspective of English in India

English reached India with ships, not with missionaries. The East India Company set up its first factory at Surat in 1613 and traded out of Madras, Bombay and Calcutta through the seventeenth century. It needed people who could read contracts, correspond with London, keep accounts and negotiate with Indian rulers and merchants — it did not think of education at all, it thought of clerks. English teaching grew out of that commercial and administrative need, and for a century it was job-oriented instruction for a small administrative elite, not a national education system.

Two documents mark the formalisation of English-medium education: Lord Macaulay's Minute of 1835, which visualised English as the language of teaching and of the higher courts, and Lord Wood's Despatch of 1854, sometimes called the Magna Carta of English education in India.

Wood's Despatch of 1854 did three things at once: it made English the medium of instruction at the higher stage, it required each province to have a Department of Public Instruction, and it established an inspectorate. Almost every English-medium institution in India descends from that administrative design.

After Independence the arrangement was never dismantled. English continued as the medium of higher education and the working language of the courts, the civil services, science and technology, and as the bridge between Indian languages. NPE 1968 gave the three-language formula its canonical form and NPE 1985 reiterated it. NCF 2005 shifted the balance: the medium in government schools should be the mother tongue or the local/regional language, English-medium schools were to be converted gradually, and English would remain a subject and a link language. NEP 2020 took this further, allowing English (or an Indian language) as the medium from the foundational stage wherever possible and keeping the mother tongue or local/regional language as the medium at least till Grade 5.

1.2 English Around Us; English as a Global Language

Take a day in the life of an ordinary family in Guwahati. English appears without announcing itself. The child reads the instructions on a medicine bottle, recognises a brand name on a television channel, listens to a safety announcement at a railway station, checks a weather forecast, types a password, sends a message to a cousin in Dubai. Almost none of this is "English lesson" material, but all of it is English. This is what "English around us" means: English has become a utility of daily life through print, signage and screens, and the child acquires it incidentally long before anyone teaches it formally.

Globally, English is the most widely learned second language in history — the working language of international organisations, science publishing, aviation, the internet and entertainment. It is the only language with a genuinely global presence, because it grew as the language of a colonial power in several continents and was then adopted by speakers who had no colonial stake in it. For an Indian teacher this matters: English is not simply the "language of the former ruler", but also the language African, South-East Asian and South American speakers chose for their own reasons.

1.3 Place of English in the School Curriculum: NCF and NEP

For most of the twentieth century a child in India who wanted to reach university had to study through the medium of English. NCF 2005 and NEP 2020 articulate the new consensus. The direction is not anti-English; it is against English displacing the language of the child's life at the stage where the child is still learning to read and to think.

NEP 2020 extends the NCF logic with a foundational stage covering ages 3 to 8, in which an English-medium or an Indian-language-medium environment may be provided wherever possible, with the mother tongue or local/regional language as the medium at least until Grade 5 and preferably till Grade 8. The National Curriculum for Foundational Stage for School Education (NCF-FS), 2022 operationalises this with goals in three language areas: W (native / local language / mother tongue), L1 (a familiar language such as a tribal language) and L2 (a further language, often but not necessarily English). English appears early as a subject alongside the medium of instruction, not as a replacement for it.

Rejecting a correct answer in Assamese teaches a child that the language in which he thinks is worthless.

1.4 Constitutional Provision; Associate Official Language; the Three-Language Formula

Under Part XVII of the Constitution of India, dealing with Languages, the language of the Union is Hindi in Devanagari script; the official purposes of the Union may, however, be carried on in English as well. The Constitution does not name English an "official language" in the same way Hindi is named — it names it the Associate Official language. The Eighth Schedule lists 22 languages recognised by the Constitution and gives them special status, though English is not one of them; the Official Languages Act, 1963 provides the working rules and created the Commission of Official Languages.

Common Mistake

English is not the national language of India. India has no national language. Hindi is the official language of the Union; English is an Associate Official language for official purposes.

The three-language formula belongs to the education system rather than to the Constitution, but it has been policy since 1968. In its earliest NCERT version every child was to learn the mother tongue (or regional language), a language from the "other" regional group, and English. In the NEP 2020 formulation the three languages are: the mother tongue or home/local language; the local/regional language or another Indian language; and a foreign or additional language — with English occupying the third slot, or being introduced early as a bridge, depending on the state.

1.5 L1, L2 and Foreign Language in the Context of English

L1, L2 and foreign language are useful as descriptions of a particular child's linguistic situation at a particular moment, not as three permanently separate boxes. The first language (L1) is the language a child understands and speaks best, normally acquired at home before school; it is the language in which the child thinks and counts. The second language (L2) is learnt after the first and used for wider purposes — school, marketplace, media. The foreign language is not used in the immediate community and has no colonial or administrative history in the country.

Now place English in that scheme. In an English-medium school in Assam, a child who arrives speaking Assamese is learning English as an L2 — second in order of acquisition, and the one the school uses. In an Assamese-medium school, a child meets English as a foreign language in the everyday sense, though because English comes with books, examinations and administrative work it behaves more like an L2. French or German in either school is a foreign language in the strict sense: no home use, no community use, no administrative use.

The home language is an asset, not a deficiency. Additive multilingualism means a new language is added to what the child already has, so that she ends up with more languages, not fewer; subtractive bilingualism means a new language replaces the first, which for a child still acquiring concepts is a loss. NEP 2020 and NCF 2005 both stand on the additive side.

2.1 Language as Communication and Thinking; the Language-Rich Classroom

Language does two jobs at once. As a means of communication, it lets one person share an idea or a feeling with another. As a means of thinking, it lets a person work inside an idea at all: a child who has no word for "compare" cannot easily hold two objects side by side and judge between them. Education is limited by the conceptual apparatus of the language in which it is done, which is why the medium of instruction is a policy question and not only an administrative one.

Language richness is not the amount of English spoken by the teacher. It is the density of meaningful language that children hear, use and have to think in, across the day and across the subjects.

2.2.1 Listening with Comprehension

Listening is the most neglected skill in the primary classroom, and the most important. It is also the hardest, because a listener has no time to look a word up: by the time the meaning of a sentence clicks, three more clauses have passed. What makes listening possible is prediction — the listener uses context, situation, topic and grammatical expectation to anticipate what is coming, and so is never really lost.

For a primary teacher, listening with comprehension means building graded tasks. Global listening asks what the whole talk is about — "Is the speaker complaining or apologising?" Selective listening asks for specific items — "How many people are mentioned? What time is the train?" Detailed listening requires the fine grain — "Why was the bag left on the chair?" A child who can only do global listening is not ready for a story, but is ready for an announcement.

Three habits make a teacher a better listener-model, and children copy the habits. Signal the structure first: "Listen. I am going to tell you three things." Signal the boundaries: pause at the end of each part. Signal the key word: stress it and write it on the board as you speak. Above all, teach the class the phrase "Could you say that again, please?" as a legitimate, confident move rather than a failure.

2.2.2 Segmental Features: the Sound System of English (IPA)

Every stretch of spoken English is built out of a small, fixed inventory of sounds. A segment is the smallest unit of a language that can make a difference of meaning; in phonetics the segments of speech are the phonemes. English has 44 phonemes: 24 consonants (17 obstruents + 3 nasals + 4 approximants) and 20 vowels (12 monophthongs and 8 diphthongs).

The phoneme is defined not by its sound alone but by the contrast it makes: two phones count as different phonemes only if substituting one for the other can change the meaning of a word. That is why English sound is analysed phonemically, and why it is taught with the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) — a single agreed set of symbols that removes the ambiguities of ordinary spelling.

Definition

Phoneme: the smallest unit of sound that can change the meaning of a word. Phone: the actual sound produced, which varies with accent and context. Minimal pair: two words differing in exactly one phoneme but in meaning (ship /ʃɪp/ – sheep /ʃiːp/). Allophone: a predictable variant of a phoneme, not a separate one (the clear l of light and the dark l of milk are both /l/). Segmental features are carried by individual phonemes — manner (plosive, fricative, affricate, nasal, approximant) and place (labial, dental, alveolar, postalveolar, palatal, velar, glottal), with voicing, aspiration and length. Suprasegmental features (2.2.3) operate over stretches longer than one phoneme.

English vowels are taught as twelve pure vowels (monophthongs) and eight gliding vowels (diphthongs). The monophthongs are plotted in a quadrilateral by height (close to open) and backness (front to back). The symbols /æ/ ("ash") and /ɑ/ (open o) are peculiar to English.

English uses the same sound resources to build differences in voicing (/p/–/b/), place (/m/–/n/), manner (/s/–/ʃ/), length (/ɪ/–/iː/) and lip-rounding (/ɒ/–/ɔː/) at the core of its sound system. Each pair below differs in exactly one phoneme; if the two words of a pair are not distinguished, the listener hears a word, not a meaning.

Two points about the symbols are worth remembering. /ʒ/ is marginal in English: it appears in a handful of loanwords — measure, vision, garage, usual, genre, television — and in a few compounds such as show and routeau, but it is not a productive phoneme of native English. And /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ are single affricate phonemes, while /tr/ and /dr/ are consonant clusters, not single phonemes.

Key Idea — an Assamese asset

Assamese already distinguishes an aspirated series /pː/, /tː/, /kː/ alongside unaspirated and voiced stops — a three-way contrast English does not have. Point to the Assamese contrast and say simply that English uses only the unaspirated member. Assamese also has retroflex consonants (/ʈ/, /ɒ/, /ɳ/) that English lacks, so warn against substituting retroflexion for English /t/, /d/, /n/, /ʃ/. The standard difficulties run the other way: Assamese has no /v/, /z/, /ʃ/ or /ʒ/, so very, zoo, ship and measure are the classic substitution targets.

2.2.3 Suprasegmental Features: Stress, Rhythm, Intonation

Two utterances can be built from exactly the same phonemes and still mean different things. "I said the sheep is fine" uses the same phonemes as "I said the ship is fine"; the difference is a fall of pitch on one stressed syllable. Features that work this way — over stretches of speech rather than over single sounds — are called suprasegmental features, and they carry as much meaning as the consonants and vowels do. In fact they usually carry more: a child's errors here make the listener stumble far more often than errors in individual phonemes.

Stress

Word stress tells the listener which syllable carries the identity of a word: normally the first in a two-syllable noun (PHO·to, TA·ble), the second in a two-syllable verb (be·GIN). In compounds the primary stress is on the first element and the second is reduced: BLACKboard, book·SELLER. Sentence stress decides which word is the point of it. "I saw the cat" is a flat statement until one word is promoted: the CAT answers "What did you see?", I SAW it answers "Did you see it?" The unstressed words become the grammatical scaffolding — particles, pronouns, auxiliaries — which is why native speakers can drop them and learners, who do not hear the difference, cannot.

Rhythm

English is a stress-timed language: its timing is built on stressed syllables, and the unstressed syllables between them are compressed to make room. Ask someone to say "I want to go to the shop today" and you will get four or five strong beats — I want to GO to the SHOP toDAY — with the small words rushed. Assamese and Hindi, like most Indian languages, are syllable-timed: every syllable gets roughly equal time. This is one of the most audible differences between an Assamese L1 speaker's English and a native speaker's, and it is a matter of timing, not of accent.

Classroom Connection

Rhythm is best taught with clapping. Say "I want to go to the shop today" while the class claps on GO, SHOP and DAY only, then again with a clap on every syllable; the contrast lands immediately. Song and poetry carry the same work — a familiar English rhyme, or a jhumur or borgeet line given in English, teaches stress and rhythm together without a rule in sight.

Intonation

Intonation is the movement of pitch over a stretch of speech, and it does three jobs: it marks a boundary, it marks the grammatical role of the unit (question versus statement), and it signals the speaker's attitude. Four patterns cover the great majority of classroom speech.

  • Fall — high to low. The unit is complete and the speaker is not asking for anything. "Close the window." "The shop opens at nine."
  • Rise — low to high. A yes/no question, an offer, or a request for confirmation. "Are you ready?" "Shall we begin?"
  • Fall–rise — fall then a late rise. Sharing information, surprise, checking a shared understanding. "But we finished it already!"
  • Rise–fall — rise then a sharp fall. Warning, insistence, or a question that carries "I mean what I say". "Don't touch that."

2.3 Oral Presentation and Oral Discourses

An oral discourse is a piece of spoken language with a purpose, an audience and a shape — a speech at an assembly, a telephone announcement, a news bulletin, a job interview, a shop advertisement, a debate turn. Unlike a casual conversation it is planned, and unlike writing it must work on two channels at once: the words and the delivery. For a student-teacher, constructing oral discourses is doubly useful, because the same text, delivered to a class, teaches children how to organise and deliver speech themselves.

Two things distinguish a good oral presentation from a recited paragraph. The first is signposting — the speaker tells the audience where the talk is going: "First I shall explain… next… and finally…", carried on three cards or a chart with three boxes. The second is support: a claim is much stronger with an example, a number, a quotation or a short narrative attached to it. In a two-minute talk, one point, one illustration and a closing line is a complete performance.

2.4 Opportunities to Use Language in Context

Language is acquired in use, and children will use English in a primary classroom if and only if using English is the easy way to get something done. Two design principles follow. The first is embeddedness: the English must be part of something the children want to do — a game, a task, a role, a decision — not an exercise standing on its own. The second is repetition with variation: the same language frame is met again and again in slightly different situations, which is how a chunk becomes automatic.

The richest source of context in a school is its own daily life: ordering tea at the canteen, asking the caretaker for a broom, welcoming a guest, announcing a lost-and-found item, telephoning the head teacher about a forgotten book, interviewing a shopkeeper about the price of vegetables. Every one of these uses a small, fixed set of chunks, and every one can be rehearsed until it is secure.

2.5 Introduction to Spoken English

Spoken English is the everyday spoken variety of the language, as distinct from the formal, standard or literary English found in textbooks and examinations. A child in Class III uses it for one specific purpose: to be understood by a listener. In the earliest stages almost all of a beginner's English is formulaic — fixed phrases rather than constructed sentences — because formulas are stored whole in memory and can be recalled under pressure. Grammar and lexis come later, built on top of them.

This is why "grammar-translation" fails at this level. A child who has only met the pattern "I am happy" cannot produce "I want to be happy here"; but a child who has the chunk "What's your name?" and "My name is…" can begin a conversation with a stranger immediately. The practical sequence is therefore: chunks first, sentences later, grammar last, never separately from meaning.

3.1 Reading Skills and Strategies

A reading strategy is a planned way of approaching a text, chosen because it fits the reader's purpose. The purpose decides the strategy, and the strategy decides the speed, the detail, the re-reading and the amount of guessing. The strategies below are not better and worse than each other; each is the right answer to a different question.

Reading aloud and silent reading

Reading aloud is reading with voice, so that the eyes, the ear and the mouth work together on the same text. Its value is not that it is louder: it forces the reader to pronounce rather than to guess, it puts punctuation and phrasing under the reader's control, and it makes the text public so that the class hears and discusses it together. It is, however, a receptive-public skill and does not by itself build silent comprehension.

The weakness of traditional "round-and-round reading" is that the whole class waits, the fluent reader carries the laggards, and the laggards stop looking at the words and start watching the mouth. Three corrections make it work: read a short paragraph and stop for a real purpose ("Why did he do that?"); give every child a turn in a pair so nobody is a passenger; and follow the read-aloud with a silent, independent task on the same text.

Silent reading is reading for oneself, without voice. It is faster, it frees the reader to re-read and to skip, and it is the mode in which real comprehension happens because nothing has to be performed. But silent reading is invisible, so it must be given a purpose and a product: a question to answer, a summary of three sentences, something to find, a number of minutes.

Intensive and extensive reading

Intensive reading is close, slow, careful reading of a short text to extract every bit of the meaning — vocabulary, structures, argument, tone, the writer's attitude. Extensive reading is wide, rapid, largely uninterrupted reading of many easy texts, undertaken for fluency, vocabulary growth and the sheer habit of reading. A class that reads four or five short, easy texts a week is doing extensive reading, and its reading rate, comprehension and writing will improve faster for it.

Skimming and scanning

Skimming is reading fast to get the general idea of a text, without stopping for unfamiliar words. The eyes move in a zig-zag: the first line of each paragraph, the headings and the last line get attention, the rest is caught in a single sweep. The reader leaves with the topic, the overall organisation and the main point. Skimming suits a chapter before a test, and the first ten seconds of looking at any unfamiliar text.

Scanning is searching a text for one specific item — a date, a name, a number, a price, a definition. The eyes move in a straight line or down a column, the rest of the page is ignored, and the search stops the moment the item is found. Scanning suits a timetable, a price list, an index, a dictionary, a noticeboard or a list of marks.

Both are teachable in one lesson: put an unfamiliar paragraph on the board with a heading, a date, a place, a number and a name hidden in it; give the class five minutes to skim for the main idea, then two minutes to scan for the items. Children discover that the two tasks use the eyes in completely different ways.

Scaffolding

A scaffold is a temporary support supplied to a learner so that the task becomes possible, and withdrawn as the learner becomes able to do it alone. In reading, the classic scaffold is supported reading: a text the children cannot yet read is made readable by the teacher, not by simplifying out all of its content but by supporting it.

  • Pre-teaching the vocabulary with pictures, gesture, objects and a first hearing, before the text is opened.
  • Modelling — the teacher thinks aloud while reading, showing how a reader works out an unknown word and predicts what comes next.
  • Shared reading — the whole class reads a big book or a board-mounted text together, with pointers and repeated chorusing.
  • Questioning at intervals to stop and check comprehension before the difficulty arrives.
  • Story-mapping — drawing the sequence, or arranging picture cards, before and during the reading.
  • Re-reading after the first hearing, when the text is no longer unfamiliar; and differing roles in pairs — one child reads, the other tracks the text; then they swap.

The word temporary matters. A scaffold that is never withdrawn produces dependence: the child waits to be told and stops trying. A good scaffold is faded deliberately, one support at a time, and the child is told that this is happening and why.

Inference and extrapolation

Inference is the reader's construction of information that is not stated. A text says "The boy put down the bag, took out a book, and began to read"; the reader infers that he is on a journey, that he is waiting for someone. Inference is a backward movement: the evidence is on the page and the conclusion is drawn from it, and it must be available but unsaid.

Extrapolation is a forward movement: the reader goes beyond the text to fill in what has not been written. A story stops at "And the old woman opened the door"; the reader supplies the rest. Extrapolation is legitimate in literature, where the point is to see how meaning is completed; it is not legitimate in a factual or scientific text, where the correct response is "the text does not say".

Coherence and cohesion

Definition

Coherence: the overall semantic and logical meaningfulness of a text — the fit between the ideas, whether or not any signals are used. Cohesion: the network of explicit linguistic devices — reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, lexical repetition, parallelism, deictic reference — that marks how the parts of a text relate.

Coherence is a property of the text as a whole; cohesion is the set of devices that signal those relations. A text can be perfectly cohesive and still incoherent (well-connected sentences about unrelated topics), and it can be coherent with almost no cohesive devices (a picture book with few words).

3.2 Text Types and Their Features

Texts fall into four broad rhetorical types according to what their writer is doing: describing something, telling about something, explaining something, or persuading about something. Once a reader can name the type, the type tells the reader what to expect — and expectation is most of reading speed. A reader who knows a text is a notice knows not to look for a story in it; a reader who knows it is an argument knows to look for a claim and a reason. Alongside these run three rhetorical appeals: ethos, the appeal to the speaker's credibility; pathos, to the audience's feelings; and logos, to logic. An advertisement relies almost entirely on ethos and pathos, a science textbook almost entirely on logos.

Worked signals of each type

  • Descriptive — "Behind the kitchen the ground sloped down to a brick wall, and the slope was full of ferns… the bricks were the colour of tea." Present tense throughout; no plot; adjectives of colour and texture; spatial order; linking rather than action verbs.
  • Narrative — "Bhim had never seen the sea. On the morning the family left Dhubri he woke before light… when the water touched his feet he stopped, quite suddenly, and began to cry." Characters and setting; a plot with a turn; simple past with time markers (on the morning, when, then); a change in the protagonist.
  • Informative / expository — "A seed germinates when three conditions are satisfied: it needs water, air and a suitable temperature… each stage depends on the one before it." A clear topic sentence; general-to-specific ordering; explicit enumeration; impersonal third person; no opinion.
  • Persuasive / argumentative — "Water in our villages is not scarce because there is none. It is scarce because most of what falls is lost before it is used… Let us begin with the schools: one tank, one tap, one habit." A claim stated in the first sentence and defended; a concession followed by a rebuttal; direct address; all three appeals at once.

The everyday text types

Beyond the four rhetorical types, the syllabus names a set of everyday text types that a primary child meets. They differ not in kind but in convention — the expectations a reader brings to each.

  • News report — the inverted pyramid: the most important fact first, then supporting detail, then background. Headline, dateline, lead paragraph, body, quotation. Neutral register, past tense, attribution.
  • Article — a newspaper or magazine feature with a title, a standfirst, sub-heads and illustrations; may be descriptive, informative or argumentative.
  • Letter — a fixed layout: place and date at the top right, address inside, greeting, body, closing, "Yours faithfully" (unnamed) or "Yours sincerely" (named), signature. The most easily assessed of all the text types.
  • Notice — the shortest and most functional: a title in capitals, the essential information in short lines, a date and a signature. No explanation, no argument.
  • Advertisement — informative and persuasive together, with the persuasive element dominant: slogan, brand name, benefit stated as a promise, a visual carrying much of the message.
  • Biography and autobiography — narrative; a biography is third-person and written by another, an autobiography first-person and self-written. Both are shaped by selection.
  • Jokes and riddles — short texts that depend on a violation of expectation: the punchline inverts a premise, a riddle gives a definition with more than one solution.
  • Stories — narrative, usually with a clear beginning, middle and end, often with a moral. A strong source for retelling and sequencing work.

3.3 Global and Local Comprehension

Comprehension operates at two levels, and a competent reader switches between them without noticing. Local comprehension is work at the level of the word, the phrase and the sentence: the literal meaning, the reference of a pronoun, the connection between a subject and a verb, the connotation of a word, the meaning of an idiom. Global comprehension is work at the level of the whole text: the purpose, the structure, the main idea, the tone, the genre, the author's implied attitude, and what the text is doing rather than what it says.

The distinction matters because the two levels demand different questions. A child can read every sentence correctly and still miss the point of a paragraph, and that is a global failure, not a local one. It is also the failure examinations reward, since almost every comprehension question is really a global question wearing a local disguise.

3.4 The Theoretical Postulates of Critical Theory

Reading a text critically means holding it at a distance and asking questions about it that a reader normally does not ask. Four families of question are prescribed by the syllabus, and they are best taught as a set of lenses through which any text — a textbook chapter, a newspaper editorial, an advertisement, a poem — can be examined.

3.4.1 The concepts used in a text

Every text rests on a set of concepts — the ideas it takes for granted rather than argues for. Development, progress, discipline, merit, normal and civilisation do most of the hidden work in educational writing. A critical reader's first task is to identify the key concepts and ask: is this concept defined? Is the definition acceptable? Whose definition is it? A chapter on "Good behaviour" that never defines good is not being careless; it is relying on a shared assumption.

3.4.2 The soundness of the argument

An argument consists of a claim (what is being asserted), the evidence offered in support, and the warrant — the assumption that makes the evidence relevant to the claim.

3.4.3 The value and relevance of the assumptions

Every argument rests on assumptions taken for granted rather than argued for. A critical reader asks about these: are they worth holding? Do they still apply today? And whose interests do they serve? An argument built on "hard work is rewarded" may be a fine starting point and a poor conclusion, because it assumes that everyone begins with the same resources. An argument built on "children need discipline" is doing something quite different when it is written by a head teacher, a parent and a textbook author, and the critical reader is expected to notice the difference.

Critical reading is not the same as criticism, disagreement or showing off. A student who writes "I disagree because my grandmother says so" has not made an assumption, has tested anything, and has learned nothing. Critical reading means the text is seriously taken as an argument before it is judged — "critical" here means careful and exacting, not fault-finding.

3.4.4 The tradition on which the text is based

No text is written from nothing. Every text stands in a tradition — of a discipline, a period, a culture, a language, a genre, or a school of thought — and a reader who knows the tradition reads differently. An extract from an Assamese oral tradition, a Victorian moral tale for children, a science textbook, a front-page news report, a WhatsApp forward and a government notice all make different claims about authority: the learned, the child moralist, the expert, the journalist, the crowd, the state. Naming the tradition explains much about the conventions of the text, including the ones a modern reader may find strange.

3.5 The Process of Critical Reading

Critical reading is not a single act of insight at the end of a text; it is a staged process, and the staging is what makes it teachable. A student who skips the first stage has usually already lost the text before it begins.

Before, settle the purpose — skim for gist, read closely for argument, look for a fact, read for enjoyment — because the purpose determines everything that follows; activate prior knowledge; and predict from the title, the headings and the first paragraph. While, active reading means holding the text, the questions and the marks in view at once. The habits are few and repeatable: mark up (underline a claim, box an example, put a "?" beside something not understood, write a one-word reaction at each paragraph break), monitor (pause at each paragraph end and ask "what has just happened, and why has the writer put it here?"), and read between the lines.

After, a reading becomes understanding in three moves. Reconstruct: put the book down and write what the text said in your own words, then check. Evaluate: the four lenses of 3.4 applied to this text. Respond: say or write what you think, in a form suited to the text — a summary, a review, a letter, a debate, a diagram, a rewritten ending. A student who can do this third move has read critically, whatever the text was.

Example

Before: "This is a chapter on water. I already know water freezes at zero. My question: does it tell me anything I don't know?" While: mark the four claims; notice that no claim has a number attached. After: "The chapter argues that everyone can save water by small habits, but it gives no figure for how much a tap leaks, so I cannot check the claim. Its assumption is that people will change their habits if told." That three-part record is a piece of critical reading, and it takes fifteen minutes.

3.6 Miscue Analysis: Errors as Learning

Definition

A miscue is an error made while reading; in the sense that matters here it is not a random mistake but the visible result of a strategy the reader is using. If a child reads house as "hose", he has picked the wrong item from a visual field in which the two words look almost identical — the error shows he is decoding by shape rather than by sound. Miscue analysis is therefore diagnostic: collect the miscue, identify the underlying reading strategy, decide whether it is productive or non-productive, and intervene. It asks not "how many mistakes did the child make?" but "what is the child doing, and is it working?"

Unit 4 · 10 MarksVocabulary in Context

Vocabulary is the whole stock of words a person commands, together with the knowledge of how those words fit together. A vocabulary is not a list but a network: knowing a word means knowing what it means, how it sounds, what part of speech it is, what it collocates with, and what it suggests as well as what it states. A primary teacher's vocabulary is the most valuable resource she can put into a classroom, because the words she uses are the words her pupils will meet.

4.1 Understanding Vocabulary and Its Extension

Two facts about vocabulary should be held together. The first is that vocabulary is the largest single component of language ability: in any continuous piece of English, roughly three to five words in every hundred carry the bulk of the meaning, and the rest are grammatical filler a reader can guess. The second is that vocabulary does not grow by being taught item by item. It grows by exposure, by rooting in a context, and by being used.

Conscientious is not learned from a list; it is learned when a child meets it in a description of a character, sees its parts (con- with, scire know, -ous full of), and meets it again two days later in a different sentence. Three encounters with a word in real contexts beat thirty encounters in a word list, because the list supplies the word without the net around it.

Table 4.1 — Four ways a teacher's vocabulary extends

Two further terms belong here. Lexis is vocabulary considered as a system rather than as a list; lexical competence is the ability to use that system appropriately. A teacher who knows a word but cannot use it in a sentence has a fact, not a lexeme. And receptive vocabulary (words you recognise when you meet them) is much larger than productive vocabulary (words you can use yourself). A child who recognises obstinate but has never used it has a receptive word; a child who uses stubborn correctly has a productive one. The two grow at different rates, and it is normal for a learner to be years behind on the productive side.

4.2 Classification of Words: Closed and Open Word Classes

English words divide into two large classes on a single principle: how readily new members can be added. The closed (or function) classes are closed because the number of items in them is small, fixed, and cannot be increased by borrowing or coinage. The open (or lexical or content) classes are open because they are indefinitely large and new members are added every day — by borrowing, by compounding, by derivation, and by invention.

Definition

Closed word class: a set of grammatical items that is finite and relatively fixed. Open word class: a set of lexical items with no known limit. Function word: a grammatical word expressing a relationship between other words rather than content of its own. Content word: a lexical word carrying lexical meaning, able to stand alone as the head of a phrase.

4.3 Categories of Vocabulary: Lexical, Phrasal and Clausal

Not all vocabulary consists of single words. A large part of everyday English is made of units of two, three, five or eight words, and these units are stored in the mind whole — as chunks — because their meanings are not predictable from the parts. Spill the beans has nothing to do with beans. Understanding such units is not a bonus; it is a large part of understanding ordinary English at all.

Definition

Lexical vocabulary consists of single content words — nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs — and of compounds made of two content words written together (bookshelf, rainfall). Phrasal vocabulary consists of fixed multi-word expressions whose idiomatic meaning is not predictable from the parts, and which have no subject–predicate structure of their own (once in a blue moon, break the ice). Clausal vocabulary consists of multi-word units that do form a clause with a subject and a predicate, and whose meaning is idiomatic (take care, give up, look after).

The distinction between phrasal and clausal vocabulary is often blurred. A phrasal expression such as a bull in a china shop is a fixed phrase: you cannot insert other words into it, and you cannot say the bull was in a china shop and get the same meaning. A clausal verb such as look after takes a subject and an object like any other verb, and its meaning is established by use rather than by composition. Both are learned whole, but a learner who knows look after knows a verb; a learner who knows under the weather knows a phrase.

Example — worked derivations

unhappiness = un- (prefix: not) + happy (root) + -ness (noun suffix) → "the state of not being happy". · unhappily = un- + happy + -ly → "in an unhappy manner"; three operations packed into one short word. · re-usable = re- (again) + use (root) + -able → "able to be used again"; a child who knows this pattern can guess re-readable and re-washable without ever being taught them. · child-centred = child (root) + -centred (adjective) → "organised around the child's needs and interests".

Compounding joins two or more base words to make a new single word whose meaning is usually predictable, though often narrower than the parts suggest. It occurs in three shapes: closed (bookshelf, rainfall, toothpaste), hyphenated (mother-in-law, twenty-five) and open (railway engine, tea garden). Some compounds have become so fixed that the parts are no longer analysed at all — inability, eggplant, cupboard. Compounding is the most immediately useful of the four processes for a primary teacher, because the child who can split a word into two parts can attempt an unfamiliar word instead of giving up on it.

A modifier is a word that adds information to another word without changing its part of speech. The normal position in English is before the noun, and a modifier placed there is a premodifier: a little girl, the blue pen, three ripe mangoes. A modifier placed after the noun is a postmodifier: the girl little with red dress, the mangoes ripe and sweet, a river flowing past the village. Postmodifiers are rare in ordinary speech and occur mainly in literary and older English and in formal writing.

Their practical use is emphasis and afterthought: a premodifier front-loads the information, whereas a postmodifier adds it at the moment of telling. Taking a simple sentence and moving the adjective from the front to the back, then asking what changes in the emphasis, teaches more about word order in twenty minutes than a rule can.

Common Mistake

Homograph vs homonym. Many textbooks use the two words interchangeably, and in an answer script that is a weakness. A homograph is a relation of form — the same spelling, usually a different pronunciation, usually a different meaning (tear, lead, bow, desert, record, minute). A homonym is a relation of meaning — the same spelling, the same pronunciation, and a shared general sense area, with different specific senses (bark, school, match, bat). A test that works: if you can predict the pronunciation from the meaning, it is a homonym; if the meaning is needed in order to know the pronunciation, it is a homograph.

Idioms are best taught through situation, not through meaning. Draw a picture of a person who is clearly ill and ask a Class IV child what the class is doing: "He is under the weather." The idiom arrives attached to the situation, which is where a child will look for it. A second-hand explanation — "an expression meaning ill" — leaves the child with two disconnected items.

Activities for Unit 4

  1. Analyse the distribution of linguistic elements. Take a passage of 200 words and count what is in it: how many function words, how many content words, and how many nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs. A class is usually astonished to find that roughly half the tokens are function words — which is precisely why function words cannot be taught exhaustively and content words must be taught deliberately.
  2. Make generalisations on syntactic and morphological properties. From your analysis, write down what you have found as a rule: "adjectives in this passage come before nouns"; "the -ly adverbs are all manner adverbs"; "the -ness nouns are all abstract".
  3. Check the generalisations against a new passage. Open a text you have not analysed and test each rule. Some will hold, some will not. A generalisation that survives several texts is a rule; one that fails on the second was a coincidence. This movement — observe, generalise, verify — is what the syllabus means by "generalisation".

S1.5 Proficiency in English – I — Memory Sheet

Essential Definitions
  • Phoneme — the smallest unit of sound that can change the meaning of a word. Phone — the actual sound produced. Minimal pair — two words differing in exactly one phoneme. Allophone — a predictable variant of a phoneme.
  • Segmental feature — a feature of a single phoneme: manner, place, voicing, aspiration, length. Suprasegmental feature — a feature operating over a stretch longer than one phoneme: stress, rhythm, intonation, juncture, linking.
  • Reading strategy — a planned way of approaching a text, chosen to fit the reader's purpose. Scaffold — a temporary support supplied to make a task possible, and then withdrawn.
  • Coherence — the meaningfulness and logical progression of a text as a whole. Cohesion — the devices that signal relations between its parts.
  • Inference — a conclusion drawn from evidence present but unstated. Extrapolation — the reader's supply of what lies beyond the text.
  • Claim / evidence / warrant — what is asserted, what supports it, and the assumption that makes the support relevant.
  • Miscue — a reading error that reveals the strategy the reader is using. Miscue analysis: collect, identify, judge, intervene.
  • Closed word class — a finite, fixed set of grammatical items. Open word class — an indefinitely large set of lexical items. Function word / content word — expresses a relation between words / carries lexical meaning.
  • Phrasal vocabulary — a fixed multi-word expression, no clause structure. Clausal vocabulary — a multi-word unit forming a clause with subject and predicate.
  • Lexeme, lexis, lexical competence — a word as a dictionary item; vocabulary as a system; the ability to use that system appropriately.
Numbers, Names and Dates
  • 44 phonemes — 24 consonants (17 obstruents + 3 nasals + 4 approximants) and 20 vowels (12 monophthongs + 8 diphthongs). /ʒ/ is marginal.
  • English is stress-timed; Assamese and Hindi are syllable-timed. Four intonation patterns: fall, rise, fall–rise, rise–fall. Three levels of listening: global, selective, detailed.
  • Committee of Public Instruction's report 1832; Macaulay's Minute 1835; Wood's Despatch 1854 — "Magna Carta of English education in India".
  • Constitution 1950 (Part XVII; Eighth Schedule, 22 languages); Official Languages Act 1963; NPE 1968 (three-language formula); NPE 1985; NCF 2005; NEP 2020; NCF-FS 2022 (goals W, L1, L2).
  • Ethos, pathos, logos — credibility, feeling, logic. Closed classes number roughly 100–150 items and are learned by absorption.
Frameworks to Name
  • Receptive skills (listening, reading) vs productive skills (speaking, writing); receptive vs productive vocabulary; additive vs subtractive multilingualism.
  • The three-language formula; L1 / L2 / foreign language.
  • Four rhetorical types of text: descriptive, narrative, informative/expository, persuasive/argumentative.
  • Four critical lenses — concepts, soundness of argument, assumptions, tradition; three stages of critical reading — before, during, after.

Course InformationObjectives, Mode of Transaction and Assessment

About This Subject

The paper is first about the student-teacher's own proficiency, and only then about how that proficiency becomes pedagogy. A teacher unsure of a word, of a sentence stress or of what a paragraph is arguing falls back on translation and rote chorus-reading; a teacher who reads fluently, listens carefully and argues clearly gives her class a model of the language in use. The units move back and forth between the two strands — proficiency in usage (sound system, stress, reading strategies, how words combine) and proficiency in classroom teaching (planning oral discourse, analysing texts of different types, doing vocabulary work that is not a list of disconnected definitions). Because this is a proficiency paper, exposure alone is not enough: the trainee must produce, be heard, be corrected and try again.

Course Objectives

  • strengthen her own English proficiency, receptively and productively;
  • explain the status of English in India — historical, constitutional and curricular — with accuracy;
  • distinguish L1, L2 and foreign language as they operate in an Assamese classroom, and plan without treating the home language as a defect;
  • develop the four basic language skills and the strategies that support them;
  • describe the segmental and suprasegmental features of English and use IPA to transcribe classroom speech accurately;
  • construct oral discourses — a speech, an announcement, an interview, a discussion — appropriate to a purpose and audience;
  • read different types of texts with comprehension, identifying the features that make each type distinctive;
  • evaluate a text critically: its concepts, the soundness of its argument, the value of its assumptions and the tradition it rests on;
  • use miscue analysis as a window into a reader's strategies rather than as a hunt for mistakes;
  • maintain a portfolio and a reflective journal documenting her work across the four semesters.

Mode of Transaction

The syllabus prescribes three strands, and all three are needed.

  • Participating in tasks and activities that improve proficiency in the receptive and productive skills — the student-teacher does, not just studies.
  • Text analysis of school textbooks to build critical literacy: the same book a child reads is put on the table and examined for its grammar of argument, its assumptions and its gaps.
  • Reflection on one's own learning, so that the trainee can connect what she struggles with as a reader and speaker to what her pupils will struggle with.

In practice the units are worked through classroom discussion, pair and group work, role play and dramatisation, recorded listening tasks, workshop-based material construction, close reading, book and article review in a fixed format, and entries in a reflective journal.

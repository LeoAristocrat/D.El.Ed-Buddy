Unit 1 Definition, Nature and Significance of ECCE

1.1 Meaning and Concept of ECCE

Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) is the collective term for all the care, protection, health, nutrition and learning a child receives from birth to the end of the early childhood years. Two official Indian documents define the span differently, and both are examinable. The National Early Childhood Care and Education (NECC) Policy 2013, issued by the Ministry of Women and Child Development, covers the period from before birth (the antenatal phase) to six years of age. The National Curriculum for the Foundational Stage 2022 (NCF-FS 2022) widens the practical school-going span to ages 3–8, because the NEP 2020 Foundational Stage links three years of Anganwadi or pre-primary with Grades 1 and 2.

Care and education in the acronym are doing equal work. Care is everything that protects the child’s body and mind — nutrition, health check-ups, immunisation, safe drinking water, a sanitary toilet, shelter, affection, freedom from abuse and neglect. Education here does not mean schooling; it means the everyday experiences through which a child learns to listen, speak, move, think, feel, imagine, work with others and make sense of the world. A programme that provides only care is a creche; one that provides instruction with no meals and no safety is education without care. Neither half works alone.

Definition

ECCE is the integrated provision of care (health, nutrition, protection, safety, affection) and education (play, language, social and emotional learning, exploration, creativity) to children from birth to six years of age, in a setting that is safe, stimulating and inclusive, and planned jointly with the family and the community.

The concept rests on five beliefs, and it is these beliefs — not the lessons — that distinguish an early childhood programme from a small school. Development is whole: a child who can count to twenty but cannot sit with a group has not developed wholly. Learning at this age is through experience, not explanation — a three-year-old learns the word cold by touching cold water. The child is an active agent who does things to the world and receives things back. Learning must be developmentally appropriate, matched to what children of that age can physically and mentally do. And the family is the first school, so care and education are inseparable and the parent is a partner, not a visitor.

Two terms must be kept apart. Early Childhood Development (ECD) is the wider process of physical, cognitive, socio-emotional and language growth from conception to about eight years; it is a developmental term, not a programme. Foundational Stage is a policy term: under NEP 2020 it is five years and ages three to eight, whereas under the earlier NCF 2005 it meant only Grades 1–2, ages six to eight, with early childhood sitting outside the school system under the welfare sector. The shift of ECCE into the school system is one of the most significant changes in Indian education policy in the last decade, and it is why an ECCE subject now sits inside a teacher-education syllabus.

One test keeps the two strands together. A meal served at the Anganwadi is care; a song about that meal’s ingredients is education. A child washing hands before the meal is a health outcome; the same child being asked “where do we wash our hands?” and then shown is the educational half of the same act. Programmes that keep the strands apart end up either feeding children or teaching them, but never both.

Key Idea

ECCE is not “primary school in miniature”. Its product is not a set of acquired information but a set of dispositions — curiosity, attention, self-confidence, the ability to wait and to try again, the habit of speaking to others, the willingness to share. Those dispositions are built, not taught, and they are what make the eight years of schooling that follow survivable.

1.2 Significance of ECCE

The significance of ECCE is best understood as a set of claims, each of which can be defended with an argument, and each of which has a consequence for what a teacher actually does.

  • Cumulative. Later learning stands on what was built earlier. A child who can hold a book, follow a two-step instruction, wait for a turn and express a need in words faces a very different classroom from a child who cannot.
  • Critical period. Language, responsive caregiving, nutrition and safe stimulation in the early years have disproportionate later effects, and deprivation in this window is harder to make up for than later.
  • Equality of outcome. A well-run pre-primary class gives a poor child language exposure, nutrition, a safe place, adult attention and peer experience that the same child would not otherwise get. It is one of the most effective levers for closing the gap between children of different economic and social backgrounds.
  • Retention. Children who have had a good pre-primary experience enter school better prepared and are less likely to repeat a class or drop out.
  • Health. An ECCE centre is among the few points of contact an Indian child has outside the home before school, which is why it is used for growth monitoring, immunisation, nutrition supplementation and early identification of disability.
  • Social-emotional and long-term economic return. A community that runs a reliable Anganwadi frees mothers to work and holds up the school system; a first-generation learner in such a family has a very different trajectory from one whose older sisters had nowhere to go.

For the classroom teacher the practical significance is the whole point: the child who has been well cared for and has been played with is teachable, and the child who has not will cost the primary teacher several times the effort for the same outcome.

Example

Consider two children entering Grade 1 in the same Assamese-medium school. One has attended an Anganwadi for two years: he can remove his own sandals, wash his hands, wait in a line without pushing, join a game with two other children, tell the teacher that his head hurts, and hold a book the right way up. The other has spent those years at home. None of the first child’s skills came from a lesson; all came from meals, routines, songs, games and being trusted with small tasks.

1.3 Domains of Development in ECCE

This syllabus works with five domains of development that recur throughout all four units: Language, Physical, Cognitive, Socio-Emotional, and Aesthetic-Appreciation and Creativity. Every activity you plan in Unit 2, every theme in Unit 3, every column of the Holistic Progress Card in Unit 4 and every practicum task in this paper must be traceable to at least one of them. The domains are not five subjects or five separate children; they are five windows onto the same child, and one good activity usually lights up several at once. Sorting blocks of different sizes and colours is physical (handling), cognitive (seriation, matching), socio-emotional (turns, winning and losing), language (naming colours and “bigger”) and aesthetic (appreciating colour and form) — all at once.

Two cautions. First, domain labels are a planning and recording device, not a theory of separate intelligences; the syllabus’s own labels are the ones to use in answers. Second, development in one domain supports the others. A child given a rich language environment and whose stories are listened to will ask more questions; a child praised for the picture he made will persist at a puzzle; a child allowed to move freely will listen better afterwards. That mutual support is the practical meaning of the word holistic.

Classroom Connection

Use the five domains as a checklist the moment you enter a pre-primary room. Ask: where are the books and are they being handled? Where is the fine-motor material? Has the outdoor space been used this morning? Is there anything on the walls that a child made? Is art material out, or is everything tidy and put away — which for a four-year-old means nothing to do? The answers tell you which domains the programme is really serving, whatever its lesson plan claims.

1.4 Factors Influencing Holistic Development: Home, Community, School

The syllabus names three settings as the factors that influence holistic development: home, community and school. The word “factor” is misleading if read as something external acting on the child, because the child also acts on all three. Read them instead as the three environments in which development happens, and ask in each case the same three questions: what opportunities does it offer, what models does it provide, what pressure does it place on the child?

The home is the first and most influential environment. It provides the earliest language — how many words the child hears, the quality of the talk, whether he is spoken to or about, how many languages in the air at once. It provides emotional security through responsive caregiving, the earliest self-care habits, and the first relationships with other people. It also transmits values: the importance placed on girls’ schooling, on cleanliness, on earning, and on what a child is allowed to do and say. Home inputs are unequal, and good ECCE practice therefore begins by enriching the home — which is why 3.4 places parental and community involvement among the teacher’s planning responsibilities.

The community supplies the wider world: the peer group, the neighbourhood children, the play space, the Anganwadi, the health centre and the vaccination camp; and also language, religion, food habits, festival rituals, crafts and occupations. Community life gives the child its first real opportunities for social comparison and its first experience of a world not arranged around its preferences. In rural and semi-urban Assam, community also includes the village council, the panchayat, local artisans, folk songs and the neighbourhood play area. Community brings risks too: it can exclude by caste, clan, gender or disability, and it can pull children into work or sibling care at the expense of play. A programme that ignores the community is working with a fraction of the population.

The school — read broadly to include the Anganwadi centre, the pre-primary section, the creche and the primary school — is the third environment and the only one fully under professional control. School influences development through what is taught and how (play and conversation, or sitting and copying), through the adult–child relationship (warm and individual, or impersonal and controlling), through the peer group, through the physical environment (space, shade, light, furniture scaled to the child), and through the daily schedule (a rhythm of varied activity, or four unbroken hours of a worksheet).

It is also the setting where the other two can be brought in — where a mother learns about nutrition, or a pond, a field, a bakery or a craftsperson’s workshop is used as a classroom. That is the case for 3.4.

Development is a product of the interaction of the child with these three settings, not a sum of independent contributions. A shy child in a talk-rich home with no peers to practise on may still be socially slow; a confident child in a large family with no adult time may be emotionally unsupported. The teacher’s task is not to compensate for the home alone, but to offer inside the programme what the home and community have not offered — and to go back to them to build what must be built there.

1.6 Agencies Involved in ECCE

ECCE is delivered by a network of agencies, not by a single system. The most important structural fact in the Indian situation is that early childhood has historically been the responsibility of the welfare sector — the Ministry of Women and Child Development — while school education has been the responsibility of the education departments. NEP 2020 deliberately changes this by bringing the 3–8 Foundational Stage into the school education system, so that the two streams meet. Any answer on agencies should show that meeting.

The family is the first agency, with the largest share of the child’s waking hours: primary care, nutrition, immunisation, language, values and affection, and — since NEP 2020 — participation in pre-primary planning and in the transition to school. Anganwadi centres and ICDS are the public delivery system: the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) scheme, launched in 1975–76 and administered by the Ministry of Women and Child Development, works through a network of Anganwadi centres delivering supplementary nutrition, health and nutrition check-ups, pre-school education for children of about three to six, immunisation, health education, referral services and care for the under-three. In recent years the Ministry has consolidated several supplementary nutrition and feeding programmes, including the Mid-Day Meal in Anganwadi, under the Poshan 2.0 umbrella.

Common Mistake

Do not say that ICDS is ECCE, or that ECCE is run by the Ministry of Education. ICDS is a delivery platform for nutrition, health and pre-school education, now operating under the Poshan 2.0 consolidation, and Anganwadi-based services belong to the Ministry of Women and Child Development; the NECC Policy 2013 is that Ministry’s document, with a 0–6 span. ECCE is wider than ICDS: it also includes home-based care for the under-three, school-based pre-primary, private and community centres, and the family’s own care. Equally, do not treat “ECCE” and “foundational stage” as synonyms.

Unit 2 Pedagogical Approaches to ECCE

Unit 2 is the heart of the paper, and everything in it reduces to one conviction: a child between three and six does not learn about the world from being told about it, but from doing something with it and then talking about what happened. The syllabus therefore opens with play- and activity-based learning, subdivides it into toy-based, inquiry-based and song, rhyme, art and craft approaches, and then turns in 2.2 to the curricular issues that any honest ECCE curriculum in India must confront.

2.1 Play- and Activity-Based Learning: Free, Guided and Structured Play

Play is what young children do when they are given the chance, the space and the time. That sounds tautological, and it is worth stating because children are not given the chance: play is chopped up in many rooms into a fixed twenty minutes of “play” in the timetable and forty-five minutes of worksheet. Real play has three conditions: it is self-chosen (the child decides what to do), self-directed (the child decides how and with whom), and purposeless in the child’s own estimate (he is not producing a required output). Strip away self-choice and you get drills; strip away self-direction and you get obedience; strip away both and you have removed play while keeping the children sitting still — which is exactly the failure mode that early formal instruction represents.

No competent early childhood teacher plans a whole day of unsupervised chaos, which is why the syllabus asks for three kinds of play. They differ in one respect only — who controls the direction of the activity — and the teacher moves along that continuum as the purpose, the group and the hour require. A well-planned day contains all three, and the movement between them is a teaching decision rather than an accident.

The three types interlock. A morning might open with twenty-five minutes of free play in which two children build a bridge with blocks and a third watches; the teacher joins the bridge-builder and asks, “how will it not fall down?”, producing a guided episode; the day then closes with a structured circle song in which every child’s name is used in a rhyme. In those thirty minutes all five domains have been served. Nothing was taught, and a great deal was learned — which is precisely the claim of 2.1.

Definition

Play-based learning is the use of play — free, guided or structured — as the primary medium through which young children acquire knowledge, skills, attitudes and values, on the principle that play is not a break from learning but the natural form of learning at this age.

Example

Guided play in an Assamese pre-primary class, with a heap of fallen leaves. The teacher brings them in, sorts none of them, and asks only, “Put them however you like.” Children sort by colour, size, texture, or simply pile them. The teacher then narrows: “Can you make three piles from biggest to smallest?” One child succeeds, another asks for help, a third says the smallest pile has “lots”. The teacher writes nothing down, but has seen who can seriate, who confuses size with number, and who persists with a hard task. That observation is the raw material of the Holistic Progress Card in 4.2.

2.1.1 Toy-Based Learning

For a four-year-old a toy is a tool of thinking. Toy-based learning has three purposes: to give the child concrete, manipulable material for concepts that cannot yet be handled in the abstract; to give the hand a range of motor experience on which writing later depends; and to give the child something to do with another child, which is the raw material of language and social learning. Select toys for what they make possible, not for how brightly they are coloured.

The selection principles follow directly. Choose open-ended over closed: plain blocks and a bucket of sand outlast a battery toy that repeats four sounds, because open-ended material serves a hundred purposes and twenty children. Choose real objects over models — leaves, stones, spoons, caps, bangles, wooden blocks, seeds, cloth, a broken watch; the ordinary material of the household and the market, culturally familiar and free. Choose large, open, safe and few: a room with thirty kinds of toy is a distracted room, because choosing becomes the hardest problem. Choose durable and cleanable. Choose matched to a developmental target: stringing beads for a five-year-old who cannot hold a crayon is a fine-motor intervention; the same beads for a three-year-old is a frustration.

Storage matters as much as selection. Materials belong on low shelves the children can reach, arranged so that the shape of the material shows where it goes, and rotated to sustain interest. Present them in trays or baskets, mixed where mixing is the point — a tray of shells, stones, buttons and caps is a sorting problem; four separate sorted trays are a shelf. One rule surprises student-teachers most: do not collect or tidy away too often. A construction the child made yesterday must still be there today, or he learns that making is not worth the effort. In many low-quality rooms the most damaging rule is the end-of-day clean-up in which the adult collects everything while the children watch.

2.1.2 Inquiry-Based Learning

Inquiry-based learning is learning organised around questions children ask, or around questions a teacher deliberately provokes, and answered by looking, handling, comparing, testing and telling rather than by being told. The syllabus names four elements: questioning, observation, exploration and experiences. All four begin with the child’s own action; the teacher supplies the material, the time, the vocabulary and the follow-up question.

Questioning at this age is not questioning in a lesson. A four-year-old’s questions are about relations — why is it wet, where did the water go, is this heavier. The teacher’s habits matter enormously: give the child time to finish the question; do not answer a question he can answer by doing; rephrase what he said so the whole group hears it; extend it with “what do you think would happen if…”; invite the group in with “has anyone else seen this?”; and accept and build on wrong answers, because a wrong answer spoken aloud and examined teaches more than a right one merely stated. The worst response to a child’s question is an impatient question in return.

Observation is both a learning process and a method: as a process it is the child’s concentrated looking — noticing that a stone is heavy and a leaf is not, that one flower has five petals and another four; as a method it is the teacher’s systematic watching, which is the foundation of assessment in Unit 4. Exploration is the extended, often messy, self-directed activity that observation feeds — digging, pouring, breaking and mending, moving things to see what happens. Experiences are the concrete first-hand encounters through which concepts are built; a field of the four Rs is not an experience, but a leaf that was picked, smelled, crushed between the fingers, compared with another leaf and stuck in a book is. Field trips and visits belong here.

The inquiry sequence a good pre-primary teacher runs almost unconsciously is: notice → name → question → handle → compare → sort → find a rule → tell someone. The last step matters as much as any other, because saying what he found out to another adult or peer is how language, pride and conceptual clarity are built together. A programme that ends at step five has produced a discovery nobody knows about.

2.1.3 Learning through Songs, Rhymes, Art and Craft

Songs, rhymes, art and craft are not entertainment between the serious parts of the day. They are the most efficient tools a pre-primary teacher has, because they carry language, memory, rhythm, movement and cultural identity simultaneously, and because a four-year-old will learn from a rhyme what he will not learn from an explanation. The oral tradition of Assam — its folk songs, khona bhat and putuna naat, its cradle songs, counting rhymes and seasonal songs — is a ready-made curriculum needing no textbook and no budget, and using it is one of the clearest ways to make an early childhood programme locally rooted.

Rhymes and songs do four specific jobs. They build vocabulary in context, so a new word is heard in a sentence where it is useful, not on a flashcard. They fix sequences and concepts in memory — days of the week, counting to twenty, the order of a story, the pattern of ba–ba–aa. They move the body, which is a prerequisite for attention. And they build belonging, because a child who knows the same songs as the rest of the group is part of the group. The teacher’s craft is in the last line: any rhyme should end with something the children do — a clap, a stamp, a stretch, a finger to touch, a face to make.

Art and craft serve a different but equally serious purpose. Drawing, painting, clay, tearing, folding, threading, block-building and model-making give the child a symbol — the ability to represent an idea that is not present — which is one of the most advanced things a small child can do. They are also the child’s first safe avenue for expression of feeling, which is why a child who is upset at home will often pour a great deal into a painting and be perfectly calm afterwards.

Two rules govern art in early childhood: the process, not the product, is the point (so never draw for the children, never correct their drawing, never say “that does not look like a flower”), and materials must be open-ended (so no colouring books with pre-drawn outlines to stay inside, no “copy this” display on the wall). Display the work at children’s eye level, not adult height.

2.2 Curricular Issues and Concerns

An ECCE curriculum is a set of decisions about what children should encounter. The syllabus names six issues on which every such curriculum takes a position: Multilingualism, Inclusion, Gender, Equality, the adverse effects of early formal instruction, and school readiness. In India these are not abstractions. A child in a rural Assamese-medium school may arrive speaking only Bodo or Assamese and nothing else; a child with a disability may be excluded not by law but by a parent’s belief; a girl may be withdrawn from pre-primary to help at home; a private pre-school may teach letters and numbers in the first term precisely because parents believe that is what “school” means. Every teacher of a four-year-old meets all six within a single week.

2.2.1 Multilingualism

India has no single home language, and most children grow up with more than one language in the air — a mother tongue, a father’s language, a community language, and now English or Hindi through television and phone.

The curricular question is not “which language shall we use” but “how do we use what the child already has”. A child talked to in a familiar language understands a new one; a child forced to receive all instruction in an unfamiliar language switches off, because comprehension is the condition of everything else. The position reflected in the national curriculum documents is that the home language / mother tongue is the medium of the Bal Vatika and pre-primary years, that the regional language is brought in alongside it, and that English is introduced as a subject to be heard and enjoyed rather than as the language of the classroom.

Concrete practice follows: greet the child in his own language first; allow him to answer in any language he has; learn and pronounce children’s names correctly; draw attention to words in the song that are the same in both languages; never ridicule a child’s accent; keep rhymes, stories and counting games in the language the child speaks best, and use the second language for the fun parts — songs, games, animal names. A very common early error is to stop a child mid-sentence to correct his grammar: a child whose sentences are constantly interrupted stops talking, and a child who talks is a child who learns language.

2.2.2 Inclusion

Inclusion means that the child with a disability, the child who speaks a different language, the child who is slower or faster than the rest, the child from a poorer home, and the child of a different caste, clan, religion or gender is present and participating in the ordinary room, in the ordinary activity, with the ordinary children — and that the room is designed so this happens by default rather than by special arrangement. The principle is not charity; it is that a group of young children who differ from one another is a resource for everyone.

Practically, inclusion requires universal design before special help: shelves and taps a child can reach from a chair, materials usable with one hand, activity at both floor and table level, activities that do not require perfect hearing or the ability to see the board, and oral instructions always accompanied by a demonstration. Where a child needs support — a hearing aid, spectacles, a helper for positioning, sign language, a special material — the resource is brought to the child rather than the child brought to a “special” room. And the last, hardest piece is adult attitude: nothing separates children in a pre-primary room more reliably than the belief of the adults in it.

2.2.3 Gender

Children arrive with a gender, but without gender knowledge, and they acquire it fast — usually between three and six, and mostly from the adults and media around them. Early gender formation is therefore largely an adult responsibility.

  1. Do not gender the material. dolls and kitchen sets for girls only, cars and ball games for boys only, books in which the girl is passive and the boy is clever. Mixed and neutral material, mixed groups, and the same expectation of every child — blocks for everybody, dolls for everybody, the same floor space — do more for gender equity than any lesson on the subject.
  2. Notice and name the sorting as it happens, gently and without accusation: “Mohini wants to drive the bus. Bishnu is playing with the doll. Shall we ask him, or will you make one too?” The teacher’s question is an invitation, not a rebuke.
  3. Intervene when adults sort children for them. This happens most often in the family, and most often as a girl being asked to help with younger siblings instead of attending, or a boy being sent to fetch and carry.

Push-down schooling is itself a form of gendering, because it is largely girls who are withdrawn in favour of academic drilling, held back for domestic care, or denied playtime in favour of home lessons. Where local culture loads specific duties onto a specific child — fetching water, minding a sibling, helping in the fields — the classroom must at least be a place where that child gets something for herself.

2.2.4 Equality

Equal treatment means the same opportunity, respect, dignity, invitation to speak and attention for every child, regardless of who they are, where they come from, what they wear or eat, what language they speak or what they can already do. Equity means recognising that identical treatment can be unfair, and giving additional support where it is needed: an extra pair of hands, an extra language, an extra month, a different material, a chair at the table for a child who cannot sit on the floor, a second helping of food for a very thin child. Equality is the floor; equity is the ceiling. A centre that gives every child the same thin blanket in January has equality and not equity.

Equality is also the content of the programme, not only its administration. Books and stories that show girls as leaders, that show a dalit child and a tribal child as friends of the main characters, that show a child with a disability playing and being liked, and that show old people, small children and people of different occupations as worthy of respect, make the ordinary curriculum inclusive without a single moral being preached.

2.2.5 Adverse Effects of Early Formal Instruction

Early formal instruction means teaching children aged three to six by the methods of the primary school — a fixed timetable of lessons, rows of desks, a textbook or worksheet, letters and numbers taught as subjects, recitation and memorisation, copying from the board, unit tests, ranking, rewards and punishments, and a teacher who is a questioner rather than a play-facilitator. It is sometimes defended as “preparation for Grade 1”, and in some middle-class settings it is what parents actively demand. The NCF for the Foundational Stage 2022 makes it explicit that the Bal Vatika and pre-primary years are play-based, that Bal Vatika has no formal textbook, and that formal instruction has no place before the age of six.

The adverse effects group naturally into four kinds.

  • Physical. Long hours seated on benches sized for adults produce poor posture, weak core strength, eye strain and tiredness, and remove the movement the brain uses to stay alert.
  • Cognitive and motivational. The play that develops reasoning, imagination and problem-solving is displaced by recitation; the child learns that the right answer exists and that the teacher finds it, weakening curiosity and persistence; and a formal curriculum pitched above her makes her conclude that she is stupid.
  • Social-emotional. Stress shows up as bed-wetting, withdrawal, aggression, shyness, stomach aches and refusal to attend — the classic school-refusal pattern.
  • Long-term. It produces a child “school-ready” in the narrowest sense and otherwise unprepared: able to copy but not to choose, to obey but not to cooperate, to recite but not to talk.

Two further arguments are frequently asked for. Formal instruction teaches the wrong thing badly: a child taught letters before six often reads by memorising shapes rather than by understanding sounds, and can look fluent without knowing how to decode. And it is unfair: children from homes where adults sit and talk with them progress even under it, while children from homes that do not are labelled slow, so a method meant to give every child a head start sorts children by family income instead.

Common Mistake

Do not write that formal instruction “damages the brain” or “reduces IQ” — those claims are not supportable. Write instead: formal instruction at this age substitutes sitting, recitation and copying for movement, exploration and talk, and those are the activities through which early language, reasoning, motivation and self-regulation are built. The defensible claim is that early formal instruction is unnecessary, developmentally misplaced and on balance harmful — not that a child who has had it can never learn.

2.2.6 School Readiness

School readiness is the ability to benefit from school, not the ability to reproduce what school will teach. The narrow, wrong definition — “can the child read simple words and write his name” — is a direct consequence of early formal instruction: the child is declared ready when he can do in advance what the class will teach him. The right definition is a set of broad competencies in four groups.

It follows that school readiness is a continuum, not a line: a child may be ready for some things and not others. It also follows that a child does not become ready by being taught school subjects early, and that a child who is not ready is a child the school has failed to reach, not a child to be ranked. What a good pre-primary programme does is remove the obstacles to readiness: makes the child feel safe, gives him a large stock of spoken language, lets him handle real materials, lets him mix with children of his own age, and lets him be away from his parent for a long day without distress.

Unit 3 Planning and Management of the ECCE Classroom

3.1 Principles of Planning a Balanced and Contextualised ECCE Programme

Planning an ECCE programme is not the same work as planning a primary programme. Nothing here is taught to a standard, nothing runs to a fixed period, and nothing is completed. A plan for three- to six-year-olds has to describe a range of experience and a rhythm, not a sequence of lessons with learning outcomes attached.

Six principles do most of the work. They are worth stating precisely, because an examiner is looking for exactly this list.

  1. Developmentally appropriate. The activity must be one a child of that age can actually do, with the materials actually available, without adult help at every step. Writing before a child can hold a crayon well, or cutting before the two hands work together, is not enrichment — it is practice at failure.
  2. Holistic. Every area of development is represented, and preferably in the same activity rather than in separate periods. A child watering a plant is simultaneously physical (grip, balance, walking), cognitive (the plant needs water; this one is dry), language (naming, describing, asking), socio-emotional (caring for something that depends on you) and aesthetic (arranging, noticing colour).
  3. Contextualised. The plan must grow out of what the children in this centre have — their language, the crops and plants of the season, the work of their parents, the festivals on the local calendar, the water and sanitation conditions of the village. A plan imported from a textbook is a plan that will not run.
  4. Play- and activity-based. Children learn at this age by doing, talking and making. The plan is written as activities and experiences, and the timetable protects time for them. A programme that is 80 per cent teacher-led is a primary programme in a small room.
  5. Child-centred and inclusive. Children participate in choosing and in evaluating. Nothing in the plan assumes a single home language, a single family structure, a physically average body or a particular level of adult help at home.
  6. Flexible and cyclical. A plan is a hypothesis. The teacher observes, notes what the children actually did, and adjusts the next week’s plan. Planning and assessment are the same loop viewed from two ends.

3.2 Classroom Management: Inside and Outside the Classroom

Management at this age is not discipline in the ordinary sense. A three-year-old cannot be reasoned with, cannot be made to sit still because the lesson demands it, and does not understand a punishment. What has to be managed is not children’ behaviour but the conditions under which the behaviour changes — the room, the routine, the materials, the flow of the day, and the number of adults.

Inside the classroom

  • Arrange the room so that movement is the default. Low shelves, open and reachable material, floor space, small mats, a corner for books, a corner for water and sand, a rough-and-tumble corner, a display at child height. Furniture that children can move is furniture that stops being an obstacle.
  • Keep materials few, mixed and open-ended. A dozen mixed objects beat forty categorised, expensive ones, because they permit more combinations. Nothing that is out of reach, nothing locked away during the session.
  • Plan the flow of the day, not just its content. Children at this age need to know what happens next. A predictable rhythm — arrival and free play, circle time, snack, outdoor activity, rest, group activity, story, departure — lowers the noise and the friction more effectively than any rule.
  • Small groups, frequent change. Whole-class instruction is inappropriate below six. Work with groups of four to six, and rotate them, so that no child waits for long and no child is permanently the leader or the outsider.
  • Say what you want, plainly, once. “Walk” is not an instruction to a two-year-old. “Walk, please — put the block down and walk” is. Most misbehaviour at this age is simply a child who has not understood what was wanted.
  • Redirect rather than punish. Move a child towards an activity, not away from people. “Let us move the water to the table” works where “stop it” does not.

Outside the classroom

Outdoor space is not the leftover. It is where large-muscle movement, risk-taking, weather, mud, insects and the widest range of play happen, and a child who is given no outdoor time in a week is a child whose body is under-stimulated. Managing it means:

  • Zoning. Separate areas for active play, for quiet play, for sand and water, for a climbing or swinging structure, and for a garden bed. Mark the boundaries with plants, edges or low fencing rather than with instructions.
  • Safe, not sterile. Safe means the fence is sound, the pond has a railing, the well is covered, the first-aid box is stocked and the emergency number is known. It does not mean a concrete yard. A fall from a low branch is part of development, and children who have never fallen are children who have never learned how to get up.
  • Supervision without hovering. Position adults where they can see the whole space. A group of children concentrated in one corner, away from the adult’s line of sight, is where every accident happens.
  • Enough adults for the number of children. The recognised ratio is one adult to about ten children; anything less turns the session into crowd control. Where the centre cannot staff to that ratio, the number of children present on any day must be reduced instead.
Remember

You manage the conditions, not the children. Change the room, the routine, the grouping, the material and the number of adults before you change the child. This is the single idea that separates ECCE practice from primary-school practice, and it is worth a sentence in any answer on classroom management.

3.3 Planning and Design of Theme-Based Activities

A theme is a subject broad enough for children to explore from several directions and a narrow enough one to hold their attention. The syllabus is explicit that activities be planned on a theme and cover all domains, and that the essence of the approach is integrated learning and learning by doing. The official themes given are flowers, vegetables, birds, water, family, transport, community helpers and animals.

Planning a theme is a five-step job.

  1. Find the thread. Start from the child’s world, not the textbook. Ask what the children have noticed recently — the rain, the buffaloes, the new shop, the illness in the street. Choose the theme they are already asking about.
  2. Set the concepts. List the three or four big ideas the theme can carry. For flowers: living things need water and light; plants can be sorted and grouped; the same word can be a name, a colour and a shape; living things are cared for. Concepts, not activities.
  3. Plan the concrete experiences first. Before writing anything down, list the things children will actually do, see, handle and say — the real flower brought in, the seed soaked in a plate, the leaf rubbed and pressed, the rhyme sung, the story told, the visit to a garden. Concrete experience is the foundation; everything else is built on it.
  4. Spread the activities across the five domains. The official example for the theme flower: exploring flowers by size, colour and smell; singing rhymes and songs about flowers; listening to and narrating stories about flowers; a visit to a flower garden; and activities for concept formation, classification, seriation, matching, colouring and painting.
  5. Sequence, and decide what you will record. Order the experiences so that each one prepares the next — seeing before naming, naming before comparing, comparing before classifying. And decide, as you plan, what you will note and keep, because that record is what Unit 4 will later turn into a progress report.

3.4 Parental and Community Involvement

At this age the parents are the first educators, and a programme that treats them as visitors has misunderstood the stage. The syllabus makes involvement a separate topic because it is not a formality — it changes what the programme can do.

Why it matters

  • Continuity. What is learned at the centre has to survive at home, and what happens at home has to be understood at the centre.
  • Language. Parents are the source of the home language. An early-years programme that does not know what language a child speaks at home cannot plan for transition.
  • Health and nutrition. Attendance, immunisation, sleep and feeding are school problems before they are health problems.
  • Trust. A parent who has seen the room and spoken to the teacher will believe the teacher; one who has only received a diary will not.

Unit 4 Assessment in ECCE

4.1 Principles of Assessment and Reporting

Assessment at this stage has one job: to describe what a child can now do, so that the programme can be changed and the parent can be told something true. It is not testing, ranking or selection. Nothing in this unit is about a score.

Seven principles follow from that job.

  1. Assessment is continuous and part of the programme, not an event. It happens while the children are playing, and it costs nothing extra. A test week in an ECCE centre is a lost week.
  2. Assessment is based on observation, and only on observation. The evidence is what a child does with a real material, over real time, with real people around. It is never what a child says he can do.
  3. Play-based, embedded, and not separated from the children. The activity that is assessed is the activity the children think they are playing in.
  4. Holistic — all five domains, no single label. A child is five things at once. A report that says “average” without saying in what is worse than no report.
  5. Developmentally appropriate, and against the child’s own previous level. The question is not how this child compares with thirty others, but whether he has moved since the last observation. At this age the gain over time is the finding.
  6. Qualitative and descriptive, not quantitative and competitive. Words, examples and a description of behaviour — not a number out of ten, and never a ranking on a wall.
  7. Used for support and programme improvement, never for selection or labelling. Records inform the teacher and the parent. They must not be used to keep a child out, and must never travel outside the centre with the child as a verdict on them.
Remember

Assessment in ECCE answers one question: what can this child do now that they could not do before? Every principle in this section is a consequence of that question.

Records and registers

The syllabus pairs the card with records and registers, and the three are not the same thing. The card is about the child; the register is about the programme.

Table 4.2 — The Three Registers of an ECCE Centre

Keeping them is not a formality: a register that is filled in retrospectively from memory is worse than one that is filled in badly on the day.

4.3 Reporting Children’s Progress

Reporting is the step that turns records into something a parent can use. A record kept in a register helps the teacher; a report reaches the family and the community and changes what they do.

Principles of good reporting

  • Report what the child can do, not what the child lacks. Begin with a strength. A parent who has been told only what her child cannot do will look for a remedy everywhere except where the remedy is.
  • Use the child’s own behaviour, in concrete terms. “He has started sharing” is a report. “He has improved in sharing” is a judgment, and is unanswerable.
  • Report all five domains, not only the one the centre is good at.
  • Report in the parent’s language. A report in a language the parent does not read is not a report.
  • Report the child, never compare the child. No comparison with the class, no ranking, no reference to how other children are doing. The comparison belongs in the teacher’s head, never on paper a family will keep.
  • Include a suggestion the family can carry out. A report without an action is a message. “Please keep talking with her in Mising at home about the stories we are reading” is an action.

Reporting takes three forms in an ordinary programme: the written report or progress card sent home periodically, usually twice a year; the parent–teacher conference held at those same times, where the card is discussed and the parent’s own observations are heard; and the open day, where progress is shown rather than described. Oral contact happens continuously, and it is worth more than the written record.

Definition

Holistic Progress Card — the official document maintained for each child in an ECCE programme, recording dated evidence of progress across all five developmental domains — language, physical, cognitive, socio-emotional, and aesthetic-appreciation and creativity — used to inform the programme and to report to the parent. It carries descriptions of what the child did, not scores.

4.4 Eight Key Standards for Quality ECCE

The syllabus asks for a set of eight key standards to ensure quality ECCE. They are the criteria against which a programme is judged, and the criteria against which you should judge your own plan. They concern the physical environment, the process, and the people.

Table 4.4 — The Eight Key Standards for Quality ECCE
No.StandardWhat quality looks like in practice
1Safe and secure environmentThe building, furniture and material are safe; the fence, the well and any water point are secured; staff know emergency procedure and the centre’s address is on record with the nearest health facility.
2Health and nutritionChildren are checked on admission; growth is monitored monthly; drinking water, sanitation and a midday meal are provided; absence is followed up and referred.
3Developmentally appropriate curriculumActivities match the age; the five domains are covered; nothing is taught formally or ahead of the child’s capacity; play and activity carry the programme.
4Learning through play and activityChildren have long periods of free and guided play, with materials they reach themselves; the teacher intervenes to extend rather than to instruct.
5Trained and adequate staffEvery teacher is trained in early childhood care and education, is present in the room and is not distracted by other duties; adult–child ratio is maintained.
6Family and community partnershipParents are known, visited, and welcomed; their knowledge of the child is recorded; the community is used as a resource.
7Inclusive programmeEvery child is welcome regardless of disability, gender, language, caste, religion or income; the physical space and the material allow for every child to participate.
8Continuous assessment and improvementProgress is observed, recorded on the card, and used to change the programme; records and registers are complete and available; the centre reviews its own practice and acts on what it finds.

Read together, the eight standards are a description of one thing: a programme that keeps the child safe, keeps the child fed, keeps the child playing, and pays attention to what the child can now do. A centre that satisfies standard 8 while failing standard 5 is documenting bad practice; one that satisfies 1–5 without 6 has not yet involved the community it draws on.

Essential Definitions

  • ECCE — an integrated programme of care, education and development for children from birth to six years, delivered through play, activity and interaction rather than formal instruction.
  • Foundational stage — the five-year stage from three to eight years defined by NEP 2020, comprising three years of anganwadi or pre-primary followed by Grades 1 and 2.
  • Free play — play in which the child alone chooses the goal, the materials and the duration.
  • Guided play — play in which an adult and the child share the purpose, the adult intervening to extend the play without directing it.
  • Structured play — play directed by the adult, who sets the goal and the rules.
  • School readiness — the physical, social, emotional, language-cognitive and approach-to-learning competencies needed to benefit from school; a continuum, not early reading and writing.
  • Holistic Progress Card — the official child-level record of dated progress across all five domains, used to inform the programme and report to the parent.
  • Theme — a subject broad enough to be explored from several directions and narrow enough to hold attention, planned across all five domains.

Important Names, Terms and Facts

  • Five domains: language · physical · cognitive · socio-emotional · aesthetic-appreciation and creativity.
  • Four elements of inquiry-based learning: questioning · observation · exploration · experience.
  • Three registers: admission and enrolment · attendance · stock and materials.
  • Official SIP themes: flowers, vegetables, birds, water, family, transport, community helpers, animals.
  • Adult–child ratio in a supervised outdoor session: about one adult to ten children.
  • Equality is the floor; equity is the ceiling.
  • Internal assessment: practicum 10 + unit test 5 = 15.

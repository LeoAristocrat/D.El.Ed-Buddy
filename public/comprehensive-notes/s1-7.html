One warning, because it governs everything that follows. A first outdoor sketch is supposed to be awkward. What is assessed is not how good it looks but whether the maker looked at the thing, made a series of decisions, and can see what they did and what they would change.

1.1 Creative Writing: Childhood Memory, Story, Poem, Role Play and News Writing

Creative writing here is not a literary exercise; it is a way of paying attention. Writing about childhood forces you to remember, and to remember accurately you must notice, because the mind fills gaps with invention unless it is held to the particular. The syllabus therefore begins where every writer begins and where no adult begins — with childhood memory — and widens into five forms, each training a different faculty.

Definition

Creative writing is writing in which the writer is not merely reporting a true occurrence but selecting, ordering and shaping experience so that it communicates something beyond the information — a feeling, a tension, a surprise, a pattern. Its opposite is not bad writing but record-writing, which is accurate and unmoved. The five syllabus forms sit at different points on the axis between the two: a news report nearest the record end, a poem furthest away, and a role play script in the middle, because it is dialogue written for other people to say.

The sensory detail method and free writing

The most useful single technique at this level is the sensory detail method: describe what you can sense, and nothing else — not what you conclude, not what you feel about it, only what a camera would record and a skin would register. Memory is itself stored as sensation, so this brings a memory alive without effort. Work through the five channels in turn: sight (colour, light, shape, what is at the edge of vision), hearing (what sound, how loud, continuous or intermittent), smell (damp earth, kerosene, chalk dust, wet grass — the most neglected and often the most powerful), touch (rough, hot, wet, heavy) and taste (rarely used, and instantly effective at dating a memory).

Alongside it sits the free-writing exercise, the standard way to break the blockage of a blank page. Take one word — rain, stone, waiting, boat — and write for ten minutes without stopping, without crossing anything out and without worrying about grammar. The product is not the point; the discovery that you have more to say than you thought is. A teacher can run this with a whole class in twenty minutes, and the sheets that come in afterwards are never the usual careful sentences.

The five forms

Story. A sequence of events joined by cause and consequence. Insist on three things: a beginning in which something is wrong; a middle in which the situation changes because somebody makes a choice, not because of luck — this is what separates a story from an episode; and an end in which the situation has been altered, even if only in understanding. Then add dialogue, because dialogue is where character appears.

Poem. Not short prose set in lines. It works by compression (one moment may take the whole text) and by music (line-break, sound-pattern, repetition and rhyme carry meaning the literal words do not fully carry). A workable sequence: choose one image, list the ten words that come to mind, then cut to three. Teach lineation explicitly — where to break a line is a decision. In an Assamese or bilingual classroom the poetry of the Borgeet (2.2) is the local model, where metre and rhythm are inseparable from meaning.

Role play script. The drama strand of objective 8, and it must be written, not only performed. A script has a title; a cast list with the number of performers; a short statement of the situation (time, place, what is at stake); dialogue written out in full, with stage directions in brackets; and a note of the props and costumes. The characteristic fault of a first script is that it explains too much and dramatises too little: cut every line carrying information the audience can already see.

News report. The outlier of the list, included because it teaches selection under constraint. It answers six questions in this order — who, what, when, where, why, how — in a fixed shape: headline, dateline, a lead paragraph carrying the essential fact, a body giving detail in descending order of importance, and a closing line. A good exercise is to write the same school event at three lengths on one sheet. The craft of the news report is omission.

1.2 Outdoor Sketch, Drawing from a Natural Object, Still Life

Section 1.2 has three parts and they form a ladder. The outdoor sketch trains you to look at the world at large and record it quickly, working directly from the thing in front of you. Drawing from a natural object — a stone, a leaf, a branch, a fruit — trains you to look at one object exhaustively and to understand volume, texture and surface. Still life, a deliberately arranged group of objects, trains grouping, light and shade, cast shadow and composition. The movement from the first to the third is the movement from the open field to the controlled one.

The difference between drawing and inventing is that a drawing is made by looking at something that is actually there. A beginner's commonest failure is a flight from the object: within four minutes the drawn tree has become a symbol of a tree remembered from a textbook. The rule of the studio is keep your eyes on the thing, not on the paper.

The outdoor sketch: kit, viewpoint, method

What to carry. Keep it small enough that you will actually carry it: a board about A4 or A5, an HB or 2B for line and a 4B or 6B for tone, a soft graphite stick or charcoal for large areas of value, a kneaded eraser (it lifts tone without damaging the paper tooth, unlike a rubber), a card cut as a viewfinder, a notebook, a cloth and a water bottle. In a school, add spare pencils — sharing supplies is part of the lesson. Colour is optional; tone and value will teach you more in the first ten sessions.

Choosing a viewpoint. A common beginner error is to sit down in the first clear space. Instead, walk round the subject once and ask four questions: What is the subject, and can it be stated in five words? Where is the light coming from, and what does it do to the shapes? What is behind it, and can I find a background that will not fight with it? Is there a foreground? Then choose the viewpoint and do not move from it. A drawing is made from one position at one moment, and defending that position is part of the discipline.

The viewfinder. Cut a rectangle about 10 × 15 cm from stiff card and hold it at arm's length. It solves the commonest compositional complaint, which is that everything in the landscape is equally important: through a viewfinder you choose a smaller, stronger slice — the doorway with the tree through it, the two boats and not the whole riverbank. As you hold the frame up, ask: is the horizon cutting the frame in half? is the main shape cut in half by the edge? can I find a third of the picture to make it bigger?

The thumbnail. Before the large drawing, make two or three thumbnails, each no bigger than a postage stamp, in three minutes. A thumbnail records only the arrangement — where the masses sit, where the light is, where the empty spaces are. If a thumbnail fails you have lost three minutes; if a full-size drawing fails, forty.

Block in before you detail. With the side of the pencil or a charcoal stick, lay in the large masses of light and dark before drawing any edge. Find the darkest mass (usually a doorway, a tree mass against the sky, a shadowed wall) and the lightest (usually the sky) and place them first as simple shapes. Only when the value structure is in place should you describe forms. The rule is big to small, dark to light, general to particular. A drawing that begins with detail will never hang together.

Work up. Now line and detail go in, but the eyes must still be on the object. Look for the edges that are firm and those that are lost — a firm edge is where colour or texture changes abruptly, a lost edge where it changes gradually. Drawing everything at the same weight is a great waste of a beginner's effort; varying the pressure of the line to vary the edge is what makes a drawing look observed rather than outlined.

Compare and correct. Before packing up, hold the drawing at arm's length and then against the object. Three things will be wrong: the proportion of one part to another, the value of a mass, and the position of something relative to the frame. Correct them. Then title the sheet and sign it with the date, the place and the time of day — that line is what makes the drawing documentary rather than decorative, and it earns marks in the practicum album.

Drawing from a natural object and from still life

Drawing a single natural object — a stone, a fallen branch, a leaf, a fruit, a bell-metal vessel — removes the problem of composition and leaves only the problem of seeing. Work in three passes. First the outline: the silhouette against a plain background, drawn as one continuous contour, since a contour that wanders is a contour drawn from an idea and not from an object. Second the mass: shade the whole form as a single simple volume, usually an egg-shape or a cylinder, so that you understand how much of the object is turning away from you.

Third the surface: only now add the character of the material — the pitted grey of a river stone, the grain of a branch, the hammered facets of a bell-metal bowl. A fourth pass, kept for a later session, is the cast shadow and the ground the object sits on. Most beginners reverse this order, drawing the texture of a stone while the stone is still shapeless, and cannot then understand why the result looks flat.

Still life — a group of arranged objects such as fruit, a vessel, a bowl, cloth — is the natural climax of this section, because it puts three problems in one frame: grouping, light, and arrangement. Three rules govern the arrangement, and they are learnable in an afternoon. Group, do not scatter — the objects must touch or overlap; a line of objects spaced like a queue reads as a list, not a picture.

Vary the heights and the sizes — three objects of similar height make a flat fence; one tall, one medium, one low gives the eye somewhere to travel. Overlap the cast shadows — the shadows must run into one another, because that is what binds separate objects into one group. Against these rules the choice of objects matters less than beginners suppose: three humble objects arranged well beat ten arranged badly.

The second problem is light, and it must be decided before any drawing begins. Fix on one light source — a window, a lamp, a bulb held high and to the left — and never use a second. A single source gives every object a highlight, a light, a half-tone, a core shadow and a reflected light, and it makes the cast shadow fall consistently in the opposite direction.

Beginners routinely omit the fifth, the reflected light — the bounce of light off the table back into the underside of the object — and without it objects look pasted on. Draw the cast shadow before the object that throws it: it is darkest and sharpest at the point of contact and lighter and softer as it runs away.

A third skill, easily taught alongside the first two, is judging distance and proportion by measurement. Hold a pencil at arm’s length, close one eye, and use its length as a unit to compare one part of the object with another. Never measure on the paper, always at the object; doing so corrects the distorted proportions that make a beginner’s figures look like children’s drawings even when the technique is otherwise sound.

1.3 Stencil Painting, Pot and Toy Making, Collage

Section 1.3 moves from drawing the observed world to making objects. The three crafts named in the syllabus — stencil painting, pot and toy making, and collage — between them demand the whole range of skills a primary teacher needs: control (stencil), three-dimensional thinking and hand skill (pot and toy), and selection and arrangement (collage). They are also cheap: cardboard, bottles, clay and old magazine pages are enough, and none requires a special studio. Because the materials are ordinary, the discipline must come from the handling, and each material has limits that must be respected. Stencil painting

A stencil is a thin sheet — of plastic, card, metal or leather — in which openings have been cut out. The design is held flat over the surface and colour is pushed or sprayed through the openings, so that the cut shape is reproduced exactly. Making one teaches a child something freehand painting does not: that a design can be planned as a shape and then repeated. The making proceeds in five steps. 1. Design — draw the motif boldly; stencil designs need fewer, larger shapes than freehand ones, because the bridges of material between them must survive cutting. 2.

Transfer — fix the design to the sheet with tape. 3. Cut out the openings, remembering the principle: the parts you leave uncut are the bridge points that hold the design together. A stencil whose openings are all isolated pieces is not a stencil but a pile of cut-outs. 4. Fix it flat with one hand, or with low-tack tape, so that it cannot shift. 5. Apply colour with poster colour, a stiff brush carrying a little colour, or a spray; too much colour floods the openings and blurs the edge. Lift the stencil only when the colour is nearly dry, and lift it straight up, not sideways.

Stencils are used for wall lettering, on chart paper for school notice boards, on cloth for bags and garments, and on leftover card for greeting cards and bookmarks. A particularly effective school use is a letter stencil for the school's own name: it makes the children designers rather than decorators, and the repeated letter teaches the value of a consistent form.

Pot making and toy making

Pot making by the coil method is within the reach of any classroom. A ball of clay is rolled between the palms into a rope; the rope is laid on a flat base in overlapping rings, each ring joined to the last by scoring and slipping; the pot is then hollowed from the top, thinned to an even wall, and the rim turned and smoothed.

Three rules govern the result: the coils must be joined, not merely stacked, or the pot splits at the joins; the wall must be of even thickness, or it dries unevenly and cracks; and the pot must be turned as it is built, so that it is not lopsided. Before use it must dry completely — in shade, slowly, over several days — because clay that is fired or painted while damp will crack. Decoration comes only after the pot is bone dry: painting, applique with small cut pieces, or incising with a pointed tool.

Toy making in the primary classroom rests on the same material logic at a smaller scale. A clay ball on a stick is a rattle; a small hollow pot with a pierced base is a pot drum; a shaped-and-dried figure with a hole in the base slides on a stick and becomes a puppet; wire, thermocol, cloth offcuts and jute can be assembled into a soft toy that moves.

The design principle is that a toy must be able to do one thing well, and the child should choose what the toy will do — sound, movement, or pressure on the palm — before choosing what it will look like. Assorted thermocol sheets, beads, bangles, wire, bottle tops, seeds and buttons are the raw material, and everything with a sharp edge must be checked before it reaches a Class III child.

Collage

Collage — the French word for pasting — is the technique of building an image by assembling found and made materials on a ground, rather than by drawing or painting it. It is the most forgiving of the three crafts and the most undervalued, because it demands no skill a beginner does not already have and it forces the two hardest decisions in art: selection and arrangement. It is an excellent thinking exercise and a poor exercise in draughtsmanship, and a teacher should present it as the first of the two.

Collage is done in four stages. 1. Ground — a sheet of paper, card or board. 2. Selection — gather more materials than you need: torn and cut newspaper and magazine, coloured and plain papers, fabric, jute, sand, stone, seeds, leaves, wood shavings, torn cloth. 3. Cutting and tearing — some materials are better torn than cut, because the torn edge of paper is irregular and lively while the cut edge is mechanical. 4. Assembly — fix the pieces from the background forward, overlapping as you go, and glue only the edges so the face of the material stays visible. Work in three groups of value — light, middle, dark — and let the darks carry the composition. The materials are the raw stock; the discipline is in the choosing.

A good classroom example for Assam is a collage of “My Market, My River” on a sheet of chart paper. Children collect labels and wrappers from their own kitchen (a design lesson in itself), torn jute from the rice sack, sand, leaves, seeds and scraps of cloth; they build the market stalls from cardboard and the river from a band of dyed paper or torn cloth. The result is a picture of their own town, and every child recognises it — which is the surest way to show a child that art is not decoration but record.

1.4 Clay Modelling

Clay modelling is the shaping of a three-dimensional form out of a soft plastic material. In a school it means modelling clay (a ready-made oil-based clay that needs no firing) or terracotta clay (a natural clay that must be dried and fired). Modelling clay is forgiving, can be reused, and is the right material for teaching; terracotta is more permanent and is what a potter uses. The method divides into two families, and every piece of modelled work uses both.

The additive method builds the form by adding material: a base slab, then coarse lumps for the masses, joined together. The subtractive method begins with a solid block and removes what is not wanted, using a wire loop, a knife or a rib. Most finished work is additive first and subtractive last — built up to find the form, then cut back to correct it. The two together are simply the logic of any craft: add to discover, cut to decide.

Armature. Before any clay goes on, decide how the piece will stand. For a figure, a simple armature of thick wire — a vertical spine, a cross-bar for the shoulders, two shorter lengths for the legs — gives the form internal support and settles the proportions before any clay is committed. Wrap the wire in torn paper strips dipped in paste, so that the clay has something to grip and the wire cannot push through. For a relief, a mask or a wall piece no armature is needed: the wall is the armature, which is exactly why a relief is the sensible first project for a class.

Building up and joining. Put on a base slab, then add the major masses — torso, limbs, head — as coarse lumps, each pressed firmly onto what is beneath. Join with slip: the joint surfaces are scored with a needle or a knife tip to give a key, wetted with slip, and pressed together; a small coil of clay run along the seam is the belt-and-braces repair. Add and join one part at a time, and check the silhouette from the side after each addition. The silhouette is the truth about a three-dimensional form — the front view flatters you and the side view does not.

Hollowing. A solid lump of clay larger than a fist will crack as it dries, because the outside dries faster than the inside. So the interior must be removed with a loop tool, a knife or the fingers, leaving a wall of roughly even thickness — about the thickness of a coin at this scale. Work from an opening, remove material in stages, and turn the piece as you go. Stop when the wall is thin enough to lift the form on one side of the opening without its sagging.

Drying. Drying is a stage of the process, not a waiting period. Dry the piece slowly, in shade, and covered, so that the moisture leaves evenly; uneven drying is the cause of almost every crack. A simple classroom method is to keep the piece in a closed plastic bag or a cardboard box for a day, then leave it out, and paint only when it is bone dry. If a crack does appear, the repair is the same as for a join: wet the crack, fill it with slip, let it dry, and scrape or sand it level.

Tools. The tool list for a school art room is short and costs almost nothing: a wooden modelling tool or a spatula, a craft knife, a small loop (a piece of wire bent into a teardrop), a needle, a rib or a shell for smoothing, a scrap of fine wire for cutting off excess, a rolling pin and a flat board, and a small container of water. A plastic spoon and a broken tile are entirely serviceable. The classic instruction to a pupil is cut, then smooth, then cut again — the tool is for shaping, the fingers are for feeling, and the two are used together.

Remember

The studio order of clay work, in the sequence it must be done and not the sequence it is usually told:

  • Wedge the clay on a board to remove air bubbles; knead it until it is uniform.
  • Decide the armature — or decide that the wall is the armature, for a relief.
  • Build the masses on a base slab, one part at a time, checking the silhouette from the side.
  • Score and slip every joint; run a coil over any seam you are not sure of.
  • Hollow the form to a wall of even thickness, or it will crack as it dries.
  • Support any weak projecting part with crumpled paper or a slip mixture while it is wet.
  • Dry in shade, covered, slowly — never in direct sun, never on a hot board.
  • Decorate only when bone dry, and fire last of all, if at all.

How Art Is Assessed: Process, Not Product

This section is not separately numbered in the syllabus, but it belongs in Unit 1 because Unit 1 produces the evidence this paper actually assesses. The 30 internal marks are distributed across performance and participation (10), assignment submission (5), unit test (5) and SIP activity (10). Not one of those four rewards a single beautiful finished object, and the mentor instruction is explicit about the reason: the paper stresses the importance of Continuous and Comprehensive Evaluation (CCE) in Art Education. Four instruments make process assessment practical. The portfolio is the simplest and the best: a set of work, deliberately kept, in which the early awkward attempts are still present — a portfolio curated to hide the beginnings is no longer evidence of anything. The observation record is a short dated note on each session: what was set, what the group did, what two named individuals did, what the difficulties were.

The critique is a class discussion in which the work on the wall is described before it is judged and the artist listens before replying; describing before evaluating stops the exercise becoming a matter of taste. The self-assessment card asks the student three questions before the session closes: What did I look at? What did I change? What would I do differently next time?

“Why should art be assessed on process rather than product?” is a standard two- or five-mark question, and the answer that scores has four parts. (i) Art skill develops through attempt and revision, so the process is where the learning is. (ii) A product judge cannot distinguish a well-taught beginner from a self-taught one. (iii) Process assessment is continuous, so it is a form of CCE and it gives the learner feedback while it can still be acted on. (iv) It encourages free expression, which the mentor instructions of this syllabus explicitly require. A fifth point worth making is that it is the only fair way to assess a class in which drawing skill is unevenly distributed — which in a rural Assamese-medium school it always is.

Key Points

  • The assessment principle — all of Unit 1 must be judged on process, not product, using portfolios that keep the early attempts, dated observation records, classroom critique and self-assessment cards, in line with the paper’s own instruction to mentors on CCE.

Unit 2 · 5 MarksHistory and Theory of Art

Unit 2 carries only five marks, but it is where the paper becomes an education rather than a hobby. Seven sub-sections are packed into those five marks, and they fall into three movements. Sections 2.1 and 2.2 are historical — the monuments and heritage sites of Assam and India, and the living culture of the Sattrias. Sections 2.3, 2.4 and 2.5 are theoretical — how we see, what visual art and fine arts are made of, and the elements they are built from.

Sections 2.6 and 2.7 are evaluative — what art is for in a school, and how art, culture, aesthetics and livelihood are bound together. Because the marks are few and the material broad, the right revision strategy is not memorisation but structure: learn the seven headings, learn the four or five facts most likely to be examined under each, and learn to argue. A descriptive question on Ajanta or on Sattriya Nritya should be answered with a mixture of fact and significance — what it is, and why it matters to a teacher in Assam.

2.1 History of Art: Historical Monuments and Heritage Sites of Assam and India

History of art, in the sense this syllabus uses it, is the study of what human beings have made and built, and of the ideas those objects carry. It is not a list of styles. A monument is a document: a temple tells you about the belief, the economy, the technology and the social organisation of the people who raised it, and a painting tells you what they thought was worth remembering. For a teacher the practical value is direct — a child who has seen a photograph of the Kailasa temple at Ellora, or of a satra gate in Majuli, has a store of real images to draw from and real monuments to visit.

Three features distinguish art history as a discipline from general history, and they are worth stating because they are the frame in which any long answer on this section sits. First, art is material evidence — unlike a chronicle, a carved pillar cannot be edited by a later court. Second, art is not only religious: alongside temples and caves there are pots, coins, textiles, toys, jewellery, tools and houses, and a child’s clay bull is no less a historical document than a colossal.

Third, art is usually anonymous or collective — very few monuments have a named sculptor, and that is itself evidence about how the societies that built them organised labour. The monuments named in this paper fall into three traditions of building, and the tradition explains much of what you see at the site.

A heritage site is a place recognised as carrying outstanding universal value and protected for the benefit of future generations. In India the two statutory instruments a teacher should be able to name are the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act (AMASR), 1958 and the Antiquities and Art Treasures Act, 1972, and both are administered by the Archaeological Survey of India. The distinction matters for a D.El.Ed student because a protected monument is one a pupil may not be permitted to draw from directly — a photograph, a postcard or a measured sketch made on the spot is often the legitimate route, and that is precisely what the practicum album and the documentation unit require.

Exam Focus

Because 2.1 is a wide sub-section, the external question is usually descriptive: “Describe briefly the historical monuments and heritage sites of Assam and India.” A five-mark answer needs four or five located and dated examples, spread across more than one tradition — the three-traditions frame above guarantees that — plus one closing sentence on why a teacher should care. A two-mark question is often “What is a heritage site?” For that, the definition, the two Acts and the name of the Archaeological Survey are the whole answer. Do not spend a two-mark answer on a description of a cave.

2.2 Sattriya Culture of Assam (Borgeet, Sattriya Nritya, Bhaona, Manuscript Painting)

Of all the content in Unit 2 this is the part most specific to Assam, and the part a student-teacher is most likely to be examined on. It is also the part candidates most often write about vaguely, so the following stays close to what can be defended from the standard sources.

Definition

A Sattra (also written sattra, sattriya or sekhra) is a Vaishnavite monastic institution of the Assamese bhakti tradition — the devotional movement that worships Vishnu in his form as Krishna, and whose great poet-saint was Madhavdeva (also called Sankardeva), working in the sixteenth century. The Sanskrit root is sthala, a place of residence or abode. A Sattra is at once a temple, a monastery, a school and a performing arts academy: it holds the deity, it houses the bhaktas who serve, it trains young boys in music and dance, and it is where the community’s plays, songs and paintings are produced and kept. The Satwa — the male devotees who perform — and the Sattriya community together developed an artistic practice recognised internationally.

Borgeet

Borgeet (“songs of Bhakti”) is the body of devotional songs of the Assamese Vaishnavite tradition, sung to the accompaniment of the khol — a set of drums of which the muri (a larger kettle drum) and the khanji (a smaller drum) are the best known, together with cymbals called khanis. The songs are performed by the Satwas standing in two rows facing each other, which is why they are songs of exchange and dialogue rather than of solo address. The great names of the tradition are Madhavdeva and Madan Kamdev; the songs are still performed today. They are short, metrically strict, and draw heavily on the imagery of the Rukmini Harana — the abduction and recovery of Rukmini by Krishna.

Sattriya Nritya

Sattriya Nritya is the classical dance form of Assam, and in 2000 it was inscribed by UNESCO on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. This is an intangible-heritage inscription, not a World Heritage inscription — a distinction examiners test. Its distinguishing features are set out in Table 2.2, and all of them are examinable.

Bhaona

Bhaona is the one-act dance-drama of the Assamese Vaishnavite tradition, developed from the Rukmini Harana story and performed as part of the devotional music of the Sattra. Its structure is unlike that of ordinary theatre, and the differences are the examinable points. Only Satwa musicians and dancers perform — there is no separate acting company and no separate orchestra, because the dancer and the musician are the same person.

There are no separate sets and no modern props; the stage is bare, and the storytelling is carried by gesture, movement and music. There is no mimetic acting in the sense of pretending to be a domestic scene: the performance is symbolic and devotional rather than realistic. The text is a formalised, metrical Assamese composition, not everyday speech, and it is recited in a style halfway between singing and declamation.

Manuscript painting

Manuscript painting is the tradition of illustrating texts by hand, and in Assam it is known by the Sanskrit word Chitralekha, the chitralekha being the manuscript painter. The most distinctive Assamese material is the barua khand, the palm-leaf manuscript: text is incised on dried palm leaf with a stylus and then darkened, and the folio is perforated and strung with a cord through a hole. The oldest written Assamese literature is of this kind, and it is illustrated — the painter’s image is part of the manuscript, not decoration added to it.

Two points are frequently muddled and are worth stating precisely. First, the manuscript painter is a distinct and highly trained person, working in a style governed by convention. Second, the Sattrias have been major centres of this art and of its transmission, alongside the manuscript libraries they built up. A student-teacher who can say that the Sattrias function as living repositories of Assamese literary and artistic heritage — preserving manuscripts, training musicians and dancers, and staging the Bhaona — has grasped the essential point of section 2.2.

The Sattria is best understood as an integrated cultural institution. Religion, education, music, dance, drama, painting and manuscript preservation are not separate departments within it; they are one continuous life, sustained by a community, for centuries. A Sattria preserves a form of intangible heritage by transmitting it through practice — which is precisely what a school does when it teaches a child a folk song the child’s grandmother sang.

A classroom activity that borrows this without needing a Sattra nearby: each child chooses a Sattria song, learns the rasa of one section, and teaches one movement of it to a partner; the class then makes a group chart recording the song, the rasa, the costume colour and the Satwa who taught it. The chart teaches the tradition, produces a piece of visual art, and puts something the children already know in front of them.

2.3 Basic Concepts of Way of Seeing

Section 2.3 is the most intellectually demanding item in the paper, and the one with a named modern text behind it: John Berger, Ways of Seeing (1972, Penguin), which grew out of his BBC series of the same name. Its argument is short and it is radical: we do not simply see. We look, and what we look at, and how we see it, is shaped by what we know and what we believe.

Definition

Way of seeing (John Berger): the set of assumptions, expectations, habits and interests through which a person looks at an image or at the world. Berger's claim is that seeing is not passive or neutral — a picture is never simply read off the retina; it is interpreted, and the interpretation comes from the viewer's own history, language and social position.

Berger opens with two sentences that are the hinge of the whole book: “Seeing comes before words.” And, describing a photograph of two naked girls as it appears in a popular magazine, he observes that “a single picture held us captive” — the image had the power to make its readers accept its framing, its cropping and its implied meaning as natural, without ever having questioned it. If seeing comes before words, the images we absorb before we can name them shape what we think we are seeing when we finally do name them.

Two further ideas complete the reading. The “invisible” factors are what a picture takes for granted and does not show you: the crop and the frame, which decide what is in and what is out; the selection of the instant out of all the instants available; the light, and the time of day it implies; the people who are not in the picture but are implied by it; the pose of the body, and what the pose has been trained to conceal; and the surrounding text — caption, article, advertisement — which tells the reader how to feel.

Publicity is the way an image is detached from its original context, surrounded by words, repeated by reproduction and offered for sale; advertising, poster-making and propaganda all belong here, which is why the SIP activity on a poster for a social cause sits so naturally in this paper.

2.4 Basic Features of Visual Art and Fine Arts

Art is usually classified by the medium in which it is made and by the purpose for which it is made. Section 2.4 asks about the two central categories, visual art and fine arts, and about how they relate. Four features distinguish visual art from the other art forms. It occupies space and is looked at; it is largely static, unlike cinema, theatre or music, though the eye does move across it; it is physical, occupying real material space that can be damaged, moved or lost; and it is a system of signs, carrying meanings that depend on the culture, the period and the viewer — which is exactly what section 2.3 was about.

Note where the boundaries lie, because candidates routinely confuse them. Fine art is that part of the arts produced primarily for aesthetic and intellectual purposes — to be looked at and contemplated rather than used. Craft uses the same materials and the same technical skill but produces a useful object, and its standard is different: a well-made basket is not a less beautiful object than a sculpture, but its beauty is judged together with its fitness.

Commercial or applied art is design produced to sell, and its criterion is partly functional and commercial. Visual art is the wider, medium-based category that includes fine art, craft and applied art, together with architecture, photography and the electronic image. Folding a poster is a visual art; whether it is also fine art, craft or commercial art depends on what it was made for.

Common Mistake

Do not say that craft is "not really art" or that commercial art is inferior. Section 2.7 of this very syllabus argues that art supports a livelihood, which means the crafts are arts. The distinction is also socially loaded: to describe the weaving of a gamosa or the making of a kaj as mere craft is to make an aesthetic judgement about a community's work that has no basis in its quality.

2.5 Elements of Visual Art (Shape, Form, Colour, Composition)

Every visual artwork, from a Khajuraho sculpture to a Class IV child’s collage, is built out of a small number of basic ingredients called the elements of visual art. The syllabus names four — shape, form, colour and composition — and they are the ones to learn in detail, but the complete studio set is longer. The elements fall into three groups: the ingredients of structure and space (line, shape, form, space, texture, volume), the ingredients of colour and light (hue, value, saturation, contrast and harmony), and the ingredients of organisation and movement (composition, balance, emphasis, rhythm, pattern). Table 2.5 gives each one with a concrete example.

Colour in detail

Three properties describe any colour. Hue is its name — red, blue, yellow-green. Value is its lightness or darkness: the same hue at full strength and the same hue ten per cent towards white are one hue and two values. Saturation (intensity, chroma) is its purity: a saturated colour is at full strength, an unsaturated one has grey mixed into it. The vermilion of a temple door, the same vermilion faded by ten years of sun, and the same vermilion diluted to a wash are one hue at three states, and saying so is a mark of a trained eye.

Colours are arranged in three orders of mixture. The primaries cannot be made by mixing others: in pigment, red, yellow and blue; in light, red, green and blue. The secondaries are two primaries in equal parts: orange, green, violet. The tertiaries are a primary plus a neighbour, giving six. Colours directly opposite each other on the wheel are complementary and intensify one another side by side; colours adjacent are analogous and harmonise quietly; a single hue worked through many values is a monochrome.

Three further points complete the section. Warm colours advance and cool colours recede, which is why a warm object in front of a cool field appears to come forward, and why a distant hill painted in blue-violet looks far away. Colour affects mood: warm, saturated colours excite and are used in festival and in advertising for celebration; cool, desaturated colours quieten and are used for calm, distance and melancholy. And value is more important than hue — a drawing in one colour can be strong if its values are well placed, and a drawing in many colours can be weak if every area has the same tone. That is the principle behind the studio advice to block in values before colour.

Composition

If the elements are the ingredients, composition is the arrangement of them — the decision about where everything goes, and the single most important structural decision in a picture. Four ideas carry almost all of it, and all four are set out in the table above: balance (symmetrical, asymmetrical or radial, the distribution of visual weight so that the picture rests), emphasis and the focal point it creates, proportion and scale, and unity and variety — without variety a picture is flat, and without unity it is chaos.

2.6 Aims and Educational Values of Fine Arts

Section 2.6 is the justificatory heart of the paper: why are fine arts taught at all? The answers are not only aesthetic, and the strongest long answer is the one that says so, because a school administrator who must be persuaded of a new period needs the wider case.

Aesthetic development — the capacity to perceive beauty, order and proportion and to judge a work, which is a perceptual capacity and not a decorative one, and which transfers to mathematics, science and the assessment of everyday objects. Self-expression and communication — a means of giving form to what cannot be spoken, particularly important for a child with limited language, limited confidence, or a home situation that cannot be talked about. Cognitive and intellectual development — observation, memory, imagination, concentration, decision-making and problem-solving, all exercised through a single activity. Motor and sensory development — hand-eye coordination, muscular control and the tactile sense, which is why clay, collage and hand printing belong in an elementary art course and not only painting.

Social and emotional development — cooperation in a group project, tolerance of other people's ways of seeing, the discipline of sharing a task. Cultural understanding and national identity — learning what one's own community has made, and therefore who one is; a direct argument for teaching the Sattria tradition and the local crafts. Discipline and patience — a large object made carefully over days teaches something no other subject does. Recreation, enjoyment and mental health — which the syllabus's opening paragraph places first of all, as the well-being and fulfilment of the individual. Creative and vocational capacity — the practical skill that 2.7 turns into a livelihood argument, and which NEP 2020 and NCF-SE 2023 both support in making arts and crafts a serious part of the school stage.

2.7 Inter-connection Between Art and Culture; Aesthetics and Livelihood

Section 2.7 joins three things usually taught separately — art, culture and livelihood — and asks who benefits from art and who is left out.

Art as cultural memory and identity. Culture is the whole of a community's life — what it eats, wears, sings, believes, builds and remembers. Art is how that life is stored and transmitted. A Borgeet preserves a devotional tradition and a musical repertoire together; a gamosa pattern carries the identity of the community that weaves it; a satra preserves manuscripts, dance, drama and music in a single institution that has been doing so for centuries.

Art functions as cultural memory: memory carried only in speech disappears within three generations, while memory carried in a song, a painting or a building does not. And it functions as identity: a child who can recognise the form of a khap motif, the rhythm of a khanji or the shape of a satra gateway knows who they are in a way no textbook statement achieves.

The syllabus asks for the inter-connection, not one direction. Art takes from culture and gives back to it. It takes its materials, motifs, instruments, stories and forms from the community, and returns a visible, portable, teachable version of that community — a poster that makes an argument, a photograph that records a festival, a play that puts a local problem on a stage. The hand magazine the SIP list asks for is exactly this two-way movement, which is why it appears there.

Aesthetics and livelihood. Aesthetics (from the Greek aisthesis, sensation) is the branch of philosophy that deals with beauty and the principles of artistic judgement. To say that art supports a livelihood is not to abandon aesthetics for economics; it is to notice that in most traditional societies the two have never been separate. The weaver producing a mekhela chador is applying knowledge of proportion, rhythm and colour while also feeding a family; the potter throwing a balanced wall is using the same judgement a critic of form would use. Traditional craft communities in India are aesthetic communities first and economic communities second, and the standard of the work is set by taste and training rather than by an accountant.

The craft traditions of Assam and the wider north-east are strong examples and should be named in any answer to this section: weaving — muga, eri and pat silk, and cotton cloth on the khap and kingaap looms; bell-metal work, associated with the Rangbaj or Rangjali tradition of travelling smiths who work and sell in the villages, notably in the Morigaon and Darrang areas; cane and bamboo work; mask-making; terracotta; dokra, the lost-wax metal casting of the Bhutia and Karbi communities; and jewellery. Each is a school of form-making, and each is also an occupation.

Machine production can reproduce a pattern faster, more cheaply and in perfect uniformity, and the market responds to price. When a printed cloth competes with a woven one the weaver is not out-competed on skill; the machine is out-competed on nothing. The effect is not merely economic: when an apprenticeship has no economic justification it stops, and what is lost is aesthetic knowledge, because the transmission of judgement was the thing being paid for. The craft object survives on the shelf and the school of making dies. Art education is one of the few forces that works against this — a child who has thrown a coil is a child for whom the hand-made object has a meaning a printed imitation cannot have.

Four moves make a strong answer: art as cultural memory and as identity, with one concrete example; the two-way relation; the craft traditions of the region named specifically as aesthetic communities that also earn; and the threat from machine production, with art education as the practical response. A weak answer stays abstract.

Unit 3 · 5 MarksDocumentation

Documentation is not description; it is the making of a record that somebody else can use — photographs, notes, sketches, descriptions, catalogues, albums. The paper asks the student-teacher to become a careful recorder of two things: contemporary artists, Indian and Western (3.1), and five specific cases — Ajanta, Ellora, Khajuraho, Madan Kamdev and Da Parbatia (3.2). In 3.1 the choice of artist is yours, so the test is whether the choice was made well and the account of it is accurate.

Key Idea

Documentation in this unit has three layers, and a good answer works at all three. The facts — who, when, where, what medium, what themes. The significance — why this artist or site matters, and what it is evidence of. The use — what a teacher in Assam would actually do with the documentation, in a lesson, in an album or in a wall display. Most candidates stop at the first layer. The third layer is where the marks are.

What is the point of learning these names? They are models of sustained practice — Van Gogh’s wheat fields, Monet’s haystacks, Husain’s horses and O’Keeffe’s flowers are one idea worked again and again, which is what a student-teacher should do with a single motif for a term. They supply images: a child who has seen a reproduction has a picture in memory, and a picture in memory is a subject to draw. And they are proof that art can be made outside a capital and a gallery — Husain never went to an art school, O’Keeffe spent forty years in the American desert, and the Sattria Satwas of Assam never left their village.

Make the documentation class produce something. Each student selects one artist, finds three reproductions, and writes a paragraph for each on the template “artist · years · what he or she made · what the work is about · one thing I could try that they tried”. The last item on the template is the one that teaches, because it converts a name into an experiment.

Ajanta Caves

Ajanta is not one campaign but several: excavation began in the second century BCE and continued in phases to about 480 CE. Add what the table does not carry — the fame rests above all on the wall paintings, executed in pigments made from local minerals and vegetable matter, and on the sculpture of the Buddha and the Bodhisattvas, none of which is visible in the rock itself.

Ajanta is worth teaching because of continuity rather than difficulty. These are paintings of people — a court, a hunting party, a dying woman — made sixteen centuries ago, and they are still legible. A Class VI lesson using a printed reproduction of the dying princess teaches more about looking at art than a paragraph of art history.

Ellora Caves

What makes Ellora remarkable is the order of the work. Buddhist caves came first, then Hindu, then Jain, and the sequence is written into the rock: nobody demolished anybody else's work, and all three stand side by side in one continuous cliff. That is cultural diversity as a physical fact, and it is exactly what the mentor instruction to this paper means by making students understand the different traditional art forms of the country. Cave 10, the “Vishwakarma”, is remarkable for the plainness of its structure against the richness of its sculpture; the Kailasa temple is the only complete temple anywhere carved downward out of a single mass of rock.

Khajuraho Group

The defining feature of the Khajuraho group is that the outer wall of each temple is treated as one continuous carved surface, so that sculpture, architecture and ornament are a single design rather than three. The largest and best-known temple is the Kandariya Mahadeva, a Shaiva temple named for Shiva as the “Lord of the Cave”.

The feature every schoolchild knows is the erotic sculpture on the outer walls; the feature every schoolchild is taught wrongly is that the temple was built for sex. The outer erotic figures are a small proportion of the sculptural programme. Alongside them are the surasundaris, musicians, ganas and the full range of Hindu iconography, and the apsaras within and the human figures outside form a deliberate pair. Write the erotic element as one part of a large iconographic programme in which the erotic is placed alongside the divine and the everyday — and remember that “Temple of Dance” is a modern description, not the temple's original name.

Madan Kamdev

Madan Kamdev — Madan Mohan Deva — was the court poet of Raghunath Singha, the Ahom king of Assam of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, and creator of the Assamese Borgeet, the devotional song-cycles of the Vaishnavite tradition; the name means literally “songs of Bhakti”. His songs are sung by the Satwas to the accompaniment of the khol and are saturated with the imagery of the Rukmini Harana.

He stands in the same devotional stream as Madhavdeva, and the two names are almost always spoken together — but they are two different people and must not be merged. An annual Madan Kamdev Award and a state organisation in his name show his place in the Assamese literary canon. He matters to this paper because he is the point where literature, music and dance meet: the songs he wrote are the material of the Sattria repertoire, performed in Sattriya Nritya and in Bhaona, and are the reason the Sattra institutions are repositories of artistic as well as religious life.

Da Parbatia

Da Parbatia is a major Sattria centre of Assamese culture at Jagiroad in Darrang district, traditionally placed in the sixteenth century, in the period of the great Assamese bhakti movement; the Ananda Vaishnava Sattra is also associated with the area. It remains a centre in which manuscript painting, Sattriya Nritya and the Bhaona dance-drama are practised, taught and preserved, together with the literary and musical culture of the Borgeet.

It therefore belongs in this unit not as a monument to a past but as evidence of something rarer: living heritage. Ajanta and Ellora are what remains of a tradition; Da Parbatia is a tradition that is still running — and a student-teacher who has visited such a centre and recorded what is actually being done there has done better documentation than one who has memorised its date.

What to document in each case is worth stating explicitly: for Ajanta, one mural described in detail — who is in it, where the light comes from, what the painter chose to show; for Ellora, the evidence of three religions in one plan and how the rock was worked from above downward; for Khajuraho, the Kandariya Mahadeva profile and how the outer surface reads as one continuous design; for Madan Kamdev, one song — its rasa, its metre, its instruments, and where it is sung; for Da Parbatia, what is being practised now — painting, dance, music, manuscript work — and by whom.

Section 3.2 is the most predictable descriptive question in the paper. Learn location, period, type, religious affiliation, chief features, technique and UNESCO status for Ajanta, Ellora and Khajuraho — the table above is the whole answer, and the dates to have instantly are Ajanta 1983, Ellora 1983, Khajuraho 1986. For Madan Kamdev and Da Parbatia learn the contribution rather than a list of dates. Do not confuse Sattriya Nritya’s 2000 inscription, which is on the Intangible Cultural Heritage list, with a World Heritage inscription.

Outdoor studies. The syllabus asks for observation of nature, landscape and colour composition. Do not treat this as a single session. Plan it as a returning practice: choose two or three places within reach — a riverbank, a market, a temple compound, the field behind the school — and return to each at least twice, at two different times of day.

The second visit will teach you more than the first, because you will see the difference between the place in the morning and the place at dusk, and because the first set of sketches gives a baseline against which to measure your own observation. For each session record four things: the date, the time, the place, and one sentence on the colour composition — which colours dominate, where the warm colours sit, where the cool colours sit, and where the strongest contrast falls.

The album on the monuments of Assam. The best version of this task is built on a visit, and even a single day trip changes the quality of everything. Where a visit is not possible, build the album from photographs and published sources and say so — a document honest about its sources is better documentation than one that implies a visit that did not occur. Structure it to read as a route: an introduction with a map, then a sequence of entries, each with a photograph or a drawing, a short note on date and place, a note on material and construction technique, and one observation of your own. End with an index.

Creative writing. Five pieces in the five forms of 1.1: from a childhood memory, a story, a poem, a role play script, and a news report. Keep every draft. The drafts are the evidence of the process, and five polished pieces with no drafts are five exercises rather than five pieces of writing.

Making a poster, a pot, a collage or a toy. Make at least one, and photograph it at three stages: the raw material, the work in progress, and the finished object in its setting. A poster for a local awareness issue — the SIP list suggests a social cause — is the easiest to make and the most useful to learn, because it forces the compression of a message into a single visual decision.

Developing an innovative Art Centre in the locality. This is the most ambitious task and the one that best serves objective 5, the aesthetic and cultural bond with society. The word innovative is doing real work here: the task is not to propose a new building, but to propose something that does not yet exist in that locality. Think about what the locality lacks.

A village with a strong weaving tradition and no display space for it; a school with a wall nobody has ever used; a Haat whose artisans sell but never explain; a cluster of children with clay and no kiln. A good proposal names the gap, identifies the existing skill in the locality, sketches a floor plan and a programme, and — crucially — shows that the community was asked rather than assumed.

Before you start, write in one page what your own locality already makes that nobody has ever thought to record. The list tells you which of the five tasks is genuinely available to you, and it is also the first entry in the album.

Activity 2 deserves a further note, because it is the one in which Berger's publicity from 2.3 becomes directly useful. A poster against a social evil is a publicity image: a designed construction with an audience, a purpose and a limited time on a wall. What makes it work is not decoration but the four questions — who made it, for whom, what does it show, and what does it leave out. The best say one thing, in a type size readable from across a road, using an image the audience recognises from its own life. A poster crowded with slogans communicates nothing, and a beautiful drawing with no message communicates nothing at all.

Activity 5 carries the longest title and does the most work. The Building as Learning Aid idea is that the school's own walls, floors, corridors and verandas should be teaching surfaces — a child walking to the classroom should be learning something on the way. In an elementary school in Assam this is inexpensive and transformative: a painted map of the district on a veranda wall, a border of Assamese proverbs along a corridor, a calendar of Sattria festivals up a staircase. The test is simple: does a child learn something from it without a teacher standing in front of it?

Back MatterCourse Information

The syllabus states the purpose of this paper in a paragraph worth reading: “The well-being and fulfilment of an individual is the common thread of the component of Fine Art and creative Drama, literary art, cinema etc. Art education aims to help connect these with the inner aspects of one's being to appreciate and create beauty and harmony within and outside. It is not about beautification applying something from outside, but an ability to appreciate the inherent rhythms, beauty and harmony in forms, relation and character.”

The last sentence is the one that should change your teaching. It distinguishes two things both loosely called “art”. The first is decoration — putting something beautiful on the outside of a thing that is otherwise untouched: a classroom wall is painted, a cover page is bordered, a chart is shaded with neat colour. The second is perception — the ability to notice that a bamboo stool has a rhythm in its legs, that a crowd on a river ghat has a pattern, that a character in a novel has a shape to the way they speak. The first is applied from outside; the second is recognised. Art education in this syllabus is about the second.

Objectives

On completion of the course a student-teacher should be able to:

  1. Develop creative self-expression through different forms of visual and performing art.
  2. Appreciate different forms of visual and performing art.
  3. Develop skills of keen observation, imagination, patience and discipline.
  4. Develop aesthetic sensibility.
  5. Develop a commitment of aesthetic and cultural bond with the society.
  6. Exchange thought and culture.
  7. Identify and develop one's own creativity and potential.
  8. Recognise the role of drama in elementary school education.

Objectives 1 and 2 are the two directions of one activity — doing and responding. Objectives 3 and 4 supply the underlying capacities, and they are the ones a school most often neglects. Objective 5 turns the enterprise outward towards society; objective 8 singles out drama, which is why role play, hot seating, character diaries and the building of stories and songs appear in the mode of transaction and again in the practicum.

Mode of Transaction

With only two or three periods a week, this 2-credit paper has to get a maximum of doing out of very little clock time. The syllabus proposes a participative and image-driven method, and its provisions should be read as a set of techniques:

  • Images (photo, painting) as stimulus — put an image in front of a group and ask questions with no correct answer: what do you see, what is missing, what is the artist telling you, what could be happening outside the frame. The image is not there to be copied; it is there to be argued with.
  • Still photographs of the students themselves — used to track a line of thought about issues. The photographs become the subject of a written observation, the basis of a discussion, the stimulus for a portrait, or the “characters” in a role play.
  • Role play puts a body in front of an idea, so a shy student who cannot write about an emotion can act it.
  • Hot seating trains listening, quick thinking and the ability to argue a position from the inside.
  • Building stories and songs shows cumulatively that a text is made, and that many people can make one.
  • Making and analysing characters' diaries links inner life to written evidence, and is often the most revealing activity in the paper.
  • Personal belongings (objects) of characters gives a story a material anchor; an object carries history, and history is what a story needs.
  • Opportunity to explore information on different topics, plus organising exhibitions and visits to places of importance — the self-study and field-exploration component.

Assessment Structure

External 20 marks; internal 30 marks as set out in Table 4.3 (performance and participation 10, assignment 5, unit test 5, SIP 10). Three of the four internal components cannot be produced by memorisation at all: a candidate who has never made a pot cannot be assessed on pot making, and one who has never taken a class outdoors cannot be assessed on outdoor studies. Hence the instruction to candidates is blunt — do the work.

Back MatterFaculty / Mentor Notes

The syllabus gives seven instructions to mentors and faculties. They are worth reading as a description of what kind of art teacher the course is trying to produce, not of how to assess:

  1. Emphasise the importance of Continuous and Comprehensive Evaluation (CCE) in Art Education.
  2. Encourage students for free expression and creativity.
  3. Work together on small and large projects.
  4. Encourage students to develop an insight towards sensibility and aesthetics appreciation.
  5. Provide an opportunity to observe and imagine.
  6. Make students understand cultural diversity by recognising different traditional art forms prevalent in the country.
  7. Involve students for innovative thoughts.

CCE is the framework and the other six are its content: evaluation that is continuous happens while the work is still soft enough to change. Free expression and comprehensive assessment are in tension unless the process — the effort, the observation, the decision, the revision — is assessed rather than the finished object. Working together on projects acknowledges that most people do their best work alongside other people. Providing an opportunity to observe and imagine is a scheduling instruction as much as a pedagogical one, and in a two-or-three-period paper it is the first thing sacrificed — it should not be. Recognising traditional art forms connects the classroom to the community, which is why 2.2 and 2.7 are in the syllabus.
